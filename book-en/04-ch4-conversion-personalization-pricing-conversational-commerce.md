# Chapter 4 Conversion: Personalization, Pricing, and Conversational Commerce

The customer has arrived — how do you get them to pay? This chapter is about how AI changes "conversion." Four battlegrounds: personalized recommendations, pricing, conversational commerce, and sales forecasting.

## 4.1 Personalization: From "Guess You Like" to "Generative Understanding"

Personalization is already the default expectation: 71% of consumers expect companies to deliver personalized interactions, and 76% are disappointed when they don't get them<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-17">[4-17]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. But traditional personalization (collaborative filtering, rule-based recommendations) hit its ceiling long ago — e-commerce has been stuck at a 2–3% conversion-rate bottleneck for more than a decade.

AI upgrades personalization from "guessing what you like" to "generative understanding." Two cases in point:

**Netflix**: More than 80% of viewing time comes from AI recommendations. A 2024 causal study (Zielnicki et al.) found that swapping the recommender for random picks would cut engagement by 16%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-08">[4-08]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. In March 2025 Netflix went a step further, starting to rebuild its recommendation system with LLM foundation models — from "predicting which tile you click" to "understanding why you watch."

**Taobao (Chinese: 淘宝)**: In 2025 it launched RecGPT, its in-house recommendation LLM with 10 billion parameters, upgrading the entire "Guess You Like" feed to generative recommendations. Official figures: clicks grew by double digits, and add-to-cart counts and time spent both rose by more than 5%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-14">[4-14]</a></sup>[official claims].

McKinsey frames the next frontier in terms of two levers: AI-driven precision promotions (offers that fit "micro-communities" more closely) and generative AI producing personalized content in a distinctive voice at scale<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-17">[4-17]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. Bloomreach's benchmarks claim AI personalization can lift conversion rates by up to 25% relative to traditional CRO and raise revenue per visitor by an average of more than 20%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-15">[4-15]</a></sup>[official claims] — vendor claims, so discount them when reading, but the direction is consistent: **personalization is moving from "segments" to "each individual."**

## 4.2 Pricing: The Era of Charging for Outcomes Is Here

AI's impact on pricing runs deeper than its impact on personalization. It changes two things at once: **how to price** and **what to price**.

**What to price**: The pricing unit of software is shifting from per-seat to per-outcome. Traditional SaaS sells the right to use software, which customers put to work themselves; in the AI-agent era, software companies can take responsibility for the work actually delivered — work previously performed by employees in sales, support, and customer success<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#1-11">[1-11]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. Salesforce is the flagship case: Agentforce's ARR has passed $1.5 billion, up more than 240% year over year<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-07">[7-07]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. HubSpot's Breeze AI agents have moved straight to pay-per-result<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-15">[7-15]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. No Jitter offers a vivid analogy: customers no longer "buy software" — they "buy work"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#1-11">[1-11]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**How to price**: The cost structure of AI products has changed. Traditional SaaS enjoyed "near-zero marginal cost to serve one more customer"; every query to an AI product incurs inference and compute costs (the return of COGS). Bessemer Venture Partners' AI pricing playbook is built specifically around charging frameworks for "a world where every token costs money"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-01">[4-01]</a></sup>[official claims]. Dynamic pricing, usage-based pricing, and outcome-based pricing have become the new normal.

The controversy around dynamic pricing is also worth recording. Delta Air Lines used AI for personalized pricing, drawing a joint inquiry from about 24 members of the US House of Representatives demanding it disclose whether it was using "surveillance-based personalized price discrimination" to push fares higher<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-16">[4-16]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. Airlines used dynamic pricing to raise load factors from 72% in the early 2000s to above 80%, while average fares actually fell — yet consumers' acceptance of "the same seat at different prices for different people" is far lower than vendors expected<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-19">[4-19]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. When pricing goes AI, technology is not the obstacle — trust is.

## 4.3 Conversational Commerce: The Conversation Is the Shelf

The third change is that the "shelf" itself has disappeared. In the past, customers browsed websites and scrolled through product listings; now a customer simply tells an AI, "I want a waterproof jacket under €200," and the AI system interprets, compares, and recommends across retailers<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-03">[4-03]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. Shopping shifts from "browsing" to "describing what you need," with AI agents executing on the shopper's behalf.

The scale figures are already striking:

- **Amazon Rufus**: more than 300 million users in 2025, monthly actives up 149% year over year, total interactions up 210%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-02">[4-02]</a></sup>[third-party verified];
- **McKinsey's forecast**: agentic commerce could generate $1 trillion in US retail revenue by 2030; Morgan Stanley expects nearly half of US consumers to use AI agents by 2030, which could add as much as $115 billion in new spending to US e-commerce<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-11">[4-11]</a></sup>[third-party verified];
- **Meta WhatsApp Business AI Agent**: opened to businesses worldwide in June 2026 — it can answer questions, recommend products, book appointments, and qualify leads, handing off to a human when it gets stuck<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-07">[4-07]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

BCG's report put it most bluntly: **a retailer's most valuable customers may no longer be human** — AI agents (embedded in Perplexity, ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and other platforms) are taking over the discover–compare–decide–order sequence<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-03">[4-03]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

The implications for operators cut both ways. The good news: AI agents are tireless 24/7 buyers that close deals more efficiently. The bad news: AI agents recognize only data, not brands — when the buyer is an AI, human emotional assets such as "brand affinity" carry no weight, and what determines whether a product is chosen is its product data (how structured it is, inventory truthfulness, price transparency, interoperable protocols)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-25">[11-25]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. Chapter 11 will go deeper into this theme.

**The conversational shelf is becoming infrastructure.** Once large retailers such as Walmart, Kroger, and Amazon launched their own AI shopping assistants, the first reaction among small and mid-sized retailers was "we don't have a technical team." Instacart's answer was to turn it into a white-label product: Cart Assistant lets retailers deploy an AI assistant on their own websites and apps to recommend products and generate shopping lists by user need, targeting precisely the regional grocers that cannot build one themselves<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-20">[4-20]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. This kind of spillover has a predictable consequence: once AI shopping assistants become a capability anyone can rent, differentiation among retailers returns to merchandise, price, and supply chain, and "having an AI assistant" stops being an advantage — **when a channel capability gets strong enough, it becomes a utility.**

## 4.4 Sales Forecasting and the Funnel: Gong versus Clari

The fourth battleground is the AI-ification of the sales funnel itself. In the past, sales forecasting depended on sales managers' gut calls: how much the quarter would close was a matter of feel. AI has turned it into a data problem.

**Gong** entered through conversation intelligence: AI analyzes every sales call, email, and meeting to extract insights — which talk tracks work, which signals point to a win or a loss. In 2025 Gong's ARR broke through $300 million, pointing toward an IPO path<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-13">[4-13]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**Clari** entered through forecasting and pipeline governance: it takes point-in-time snapshots of every change to every opportunity, and AI forecasts revenue on that basis<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-04">[4-04]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

By the end of 2025, the boundary between the two had blurred — both do conversation analytics, both do forecasting, both sell "Revenue Intelligence"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-04">[4-04]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The very existence of this category says something: **sales management is moving from "experience-driven" to "AI-driven"** — not replacing sales managers, but backing their judgment with data.

The Chinese example is Neocrm (Chinese: 销售易). In 2026 it released NeoAgent 2.0, and its president, Deng Yongfu (Chinese: 邓永富), drew a clear distinction: "AI CRM 1.0 is a CRM with AI added on; 2.0 is a CRM born for AI" — shifting from a process tool to one built around a business semantic model<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-18">[4-18]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The AI CRM research report co-published by CAICT (Chinese: 信通院) and Fenxiangxiaoke (Chinese: 纷享销客) sets out a technical roadmap: large models drive CRM interaction from "forms and menus" to "conversational interaction," and the system shifts from "humans driving the system" to "the system driving humans"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-10">[4-10]</a></sup>[official claims].

## 4.5 Beyond Attribution: High ROAS Does Not Mean High Growth

Everything earlier in Chapter 4 is about making conversion measurement more accurate. But one thing the overseas-media-buying world in 2026 kept repeating is that **"measuring accurately" and "buying correctly" are two different problems.**

The overseas-media agency GoodyAds draws a distinction: attribution answers "who gets credit for the conversion under the rules," while incrementality answers "which conversions would not have happened without the ad." The mature practice is to use attribution for daily optimization and incrementality experiments for quarterly and major budget decisions. **High ROAS can come with no incrementality**: brand keywords, retargeting, and coupon channels easily reach people who were already going to buy, and the platform is not wrong to attribute the order to the ad under a click/view window — but those orders were not necessarily created by the ad. If sales barely move after the ad stops, the attributed value exceeds the incremental value. Methodologically, teams should use incremental conversions (the between-group difference), iCPA (ad spend ÷ incremental conversions), and iROAS (incremental value ÷ ad spend), and report point estimates with confidence intervals rather than a single percentage<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-21">[4-21]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

Two classic cases show this is not a new problem. Growth Hackers Taiwan recounts: eBay commissioned UC Berkeley economist Steve Tadelis to run a brand-keyword ad experiment (later NBER Working Paper w20171), fully switching off brand-term ads in some regions, with organic search traffic barely falling; around 2017 Uber had marketing-performance lead Kevin Frisch run a mobile-ad incrementality test, cutting part of the budget to nearly zero with new-user installs barely dipping — a finding that grew into the Uber v. Fetch Media lawsuit. The article lays out three holdout steps (randomize groups, run at least two weeks, compare the two groups' purchase rates) and names four obstacles: agencies paid a share of media spend, bosses wanting pretty numbers, platform attribution black boxes, and the short-cycle culture of major shopping festivals — **many teams don't run incrementality tests not for technical reasons but for cultural ones** — and it offers small and mid-sized merchants a simplified "geographic holdout" (pick two cities of similar spending power, keep advertising in one and stop in the other, and compare sales changes over two to four weeks)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-22">[4-22]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

## 4.6 Synthetic Consumers: Compressing User Research from Weeks to 24 Hours

The data-science firm PyMC Labs offers a practical guide to "synthetic consumers": AI personas that reproduce how real people reason and make purchase decisions, usable as digital respondents for immediate feedback on products, pricing, or creative concepts, with the advantages of speed, scalability, and precision (any population, results in minutes). It cites research that, once calibrated, they can reach "up to 90% agreement" with human survey data and 85% distributional similarity in concept and pricing research, and that leading brands have already compressed research cycles from weeks to within 24 hours; it predicts synthetic data will account for more than 50% of market-research inputs by 2027. The boundaries are equally clear: synthetic consumers excel at structured reasoning such as ranking, pricing, and sentiment analysis, but remain limited on subtle emotional differences, cultural context, and group dynamics, so their role is to "supplement rather than replace" human research<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-23">[4-23]</a></sup>[third-party research].

This adds one more link to Chapter 4's conversion toolchain: **the input side of personalized pricing and concept testing is shifting from "recruiting real people for research" to "replicating real people with AI"** — attractive on speed and cost, but calibration quality and applicable boundaries must be written into procurement standards, or a loop of "using synthetic users to validate synthetic users" will mask genuine preferences.

## 4.7 The Boundary of an Outcome: When Silence Counts as "Resolved"

Outcome-based pricing moved from slogan to contract in 2026, but the hard part is not the pricing model — it is **who owns the definition of the word "outcome."**

**One contract, two algorithms.** The pricing scholar Utpal Dholakia offers a very usable example: an online furniture retailer signs an agreement with an AI vendor to pay $2 for every customer complaint "resolved," and the vendor automatically classifies "no follow-up communication within 72 hours" as resolved. The result is that two very different groups get counted as successes — "silent churners" who left for a competitor and could not be bothered to come back, and "loyal customers" who were simply too lazy to return anything; both trigger the $2 charge, but they mean opposite things commercially<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-24">[4-24]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **What makes the example valuable is that it exposes the implicit assumption behind pay-for-outcome pricing: silence defaults to satisfaction.** For any AI customer-operations billing design, the first order of business is to specify which side silence falls on.

**Read the other way, leading vendors are writing the definition down in detail.** Intercom published Fin's outcome billing definitions: $0.99 per outcome covers resolution (the AI answers and the customer does not ask again), procedure handoff, disqualification, and self-serve routing; $9.99 per outcome covers qualification (matching eligibility criteria and routing to a team). Items that are **not** billed include escalated (cannot be resolved, the customer asks for a human, or an escalation is triggered), spam, pending, and abandoned — sessions where no answer was given or no qualification decision was completed and the customer gave up<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-25">[4-25]</a></sup>[official]. Read side by side, the two documents show exactly where the industry splits: **under the same "pay per outcome" label, one side counts silence as success and the other explicitly excludes abandonment from billing.**

**When the outcome is undefined, the vendor's fallback is a more measurable unit.** After interviewing pricing leaders at Gong, New Relic, Apple Ads, and HubSpot, Chargebee identifies a pattern: AI agents can be reused across teams, so "outcome" means different things to different people and resists standardization — Zapier therefore does not price individual outcomes but charges by monthly "activity volume," describing the value as "work completed autonomously," while Decagon abandoned pure per-outcome pricing for per-conversation pricing because customer-configured workflows amplify margin risk. On the optimistic side of the same interview set, Intercom's CEO says outcome pricing pulled a company whose growth had been decelerating for 15 years back to being "the fastest-growing large software company"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-26">[4-26]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**The pricing structure is also being pushed along by AI's cost structure.** Growth Unhinged's annual B2B monetization survey (aggregating more than 240 software companies) yields three findings: about three-quarters of software companies changed pricing or packaging in the past year, an unusually high rate; AI products carry gross margins of only about 50%, far below the 70–80%-plus of SaaS, and AI spending is mainly cannibalizing existing SaaS budgets rather than creating new ones; vendors therefore point to services spending as the next frontier, packaging outcomes into fixed fees — HubSpot releasing outcome-based pricing for Breeze agents in April 2026 is the marker of a large vendor leading the pricing upheaval<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-27">[4-27]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **For buyers, this section yields two practical rules: first, write the outcome as reviewable decision criteria (who decides, how long silence lasts, how abandonment is treated); second, accept that the consideration may be mixed (platform fee plus outcome fee), because pure pay-per-outcome usually means the vendor has folded the uncertainty into the unit price.**

## 4.8 The Margin Sheet: Inference Cost Has Been Written into Pricing

Section 4.2 covered billing units moving from seats toward outcomes. This section adds the other half: **cost structure decides how far pricing can go.**

**Start with gross margin on the table.** ICONIQ's 2026 survey puts average gross margin for AI companies at 52%, up from 41% in 2024 — improving, but still well below traditional SaaS. Bessemer's February 2026 pricing handbook lands in the same range: AI services margin of 50–60% against 80–90% for SaaS. The direction of travel matters more: ICONIQ found that as products mature and usage grows, model inference rises from 20% to 23% of total spend — **scale does not dilute COGS, it amplifies its share.** That inverts the SaaS intuition of near-zero marginal cost per additional user, and explains why AI companies watch pricing structure rather than user counts<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-28">[4-28]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**The biggest cost lever isn't procurement, it's routing.** Within a single vendor the price spread is already more than 5x: Claude Haiku 4.5 at $1 input / $5 output per million tokens against Opus 4.8 at $5 / $25. Three frontier vendors converged independently on roughly a 90% discount for cache reads, but bill cache writes and storage differently — which means "put the cheap model on the cheap step" has to be built into the product architecture, not left to procurement negotiation<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-28">[4-28]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**The counter-example explains more than the successes do.** GitHub saw Copilot losing money on heavy usage — not because the price was too low, but because agentic loops consume far more tokens than single-turn chat: one agent task can take dozens of tool calls, each billed at full inference cost. In 2026 it moved every plan to usage-based billing. That is a public admission that **the billing unit has to line up with the cost unit**: in software priced per seat, meeting a cost structure that burns per token, the gap can only be absorbed by the vendor<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-28">[4-28]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**Outcome pricing is being pushed by the same cost pressure.** ICONIQ's data show 18% of AI companies using outcome-based pricing, against 2% in Q2 2025. Intercom's $0.99 per resolution aligns sales, customer success and product on "tickets resolved," and raises new questions: how does an AE quote a large deal, how much expansion revenue does CS get? **Read the three paragraphs together and the first-order pricing question for 2026 is no longer "what am I worth" but "which slice of my cost is variable, and whose action triggers it."** The answer sets the billing unit, and the billing unit decides whether margin holds<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-28">[4-28]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

## 4.9 Evaluation Became the Longest Stage

The earlier sections of this chapter covered turning intent into a deal: personalization, pricing, conversational selling, friction inside the conversation. This section takes the buyer's view and asks where the longest wait sits in 2026. The answer is evaluation, not research.

**Evaluation has overtaken research as the longest leg of the buying journey.** In June 2026 G2 surveyed 1,038 B2B software decision-makers (North America, EMEA, Asia-Pacific, from individual contributors to the C-suite) and ran 55 in-depth interviews; the report is titled The Evaluation Maze. Evaluation accounts for 40% of the buying journey, ahead of research at 36%. After a vendor is selected, the biggest delays are IT security review (39%, and 50% at large enterprises), then budget approval (32%) and implementation planning (25%). AI makes candidate lists form faster — 82% of buyers had received software recommendations from an AI chatbot in the past 24 months, and 50% of those said AI mattered most in "narrowing and comparing options" — while review sites (38%) edged ahead of AI chatbots (37%) for the first time as the top source shaping shortlists<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-29">[4-29]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Agents are inside evaluation, but they do not hold the signature.** In the same report, 61% of buyers are using or planning to use AI agents in procurement (another 19% in limited scenarios), mainly for total cost of ownership (51%), building lists (51%), researching options (49%) and evaluating shortlisted vendors (46%). Yet only 9% accept an agent placing orders directly within guardrails, and 2% accept ordering without prior approval. Financial power has moved up: finance involvement in software decisions rose from 31% to 46% within a year, and 49% of buyers saw a CFO veto an already-approved purchase in the past 12 months, rising to 54% at organizations with a dedicated token/LLM budget. Internal resistance to AI adoption rose from 16% to 29%, the largest single shift in the study. **Read together, these four numbers explain why conversion teams feel that people are easier to persuade while organizations are harder to move: the decision-maker changed, and the objections now come from inside the house.**

**One step further along, late-stage documents are becoming AI-readable sales material.** A Gartner analysis from May 2026 gives a number further down the funnel than "buyers use AI for research": 89% of global buyers use generative AI tools or agents in late-stage procurement — contract review, vendor screening, solution comparison and even price negotiation — with Australia at 97%. The implication runs both ways: buyers have to govern AI participation and avoid unbounded delegation, and sellers have to assume their proposal is first read and compared by an agent, with human sales entering later<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-30">[4-30]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Contract terms, quote structures and qualification documents, long treated as attachments, are now part of the sales material.

**On the consumer side the same curve reads more harshly.** A survey of 4,308 respondents by the Shanghai Consumer Council found that 84.56% had tried AI shopping features, but only 16.06% thought AI could match products accurately; 64.22% worried about privacy and 62.53% questioned the accuracy of AI decisions; at the same time more than 85% expected "one-stop AI shopping," with 38.65% strongly wanting one-click agent purchasing. Users do not dislike AI; they dislike the AI they have. The failures are specific: cross-platform price comparison does not work, because an AI shopping assistant is the platform's AI first and the user's second; AI try-on cannot reproduce how fabric drapes or feels; and users suspect recommendations concentrate on merchants with high paid weight, with no way to tell "smart suggestion" from a bid. The categorization worth keeping: in parameterized categories (electronics, appliances, home goods) AI replaces mechanical comparison most effectively, while in categories driven by aesthetics and self-expression users still want to keep the choosing<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-31">[4-31]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The largest live test on the Chinese side produced behavior migration, not transaction replacement.** Tmall opened sales at 8pm on October 20, 2026; four hours later 124 brands had passed RMB 100 million in GMV and 27,681 brands had exceeded their full-day totals from the previous year. JD.com opened on October 12 with double-digit growth in ordering users year over year. The behavioral numbers matter more: Taobao's head of 3C said at the launch event that views of AI-related categories on Taobao exceeded 4 billion, and more than 10 million users engaged with AI-related trends this year. A survey by the China Association of Small and Medium Commercial Enterprises with the Lixin Data Research Institute for Q2 2026 found 79.4% of consumers actively consult AI tools such as Doubao or DeepSeek when shopping, with 43.7% saying AI advice had "a great deal" or "considerable" influence on decisions — surpassing traditional media advertising at 42.6% for the first time. That 43.7% versus 42.6% comparison is the most quotable part: it marks the first time AI advice outranks traditional advertising in consumers' own accounts, not that AI has taken over the transaction<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-32">[4-32]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **Every technique in this chapter ultimately lands on the same consensus: AI is inside the decision chain, but the signature still comes from a person and a budget process.**

## 4.10 After Outcome-Based Pricing, Who Defines the Outcome

Section 4.7 covered the billing dispute over whether silence counts as resolution. This section pulls the lens back: as the industry migrates from per-seat to per-outcome pricing, the argument is no longer about converting units but about who has the right to define "outcome."

**Two companies on the same stage reached the same verdict.** At the HumanX conference, Zendesk CEO Tom Eggemeier and Sierra co-founder Clay Bavor both said per-seat software pricing is ending. Eggemeier's words were blunt — "to be clear: seats are dead" — and he put a timetable on it: the shift takes three to five years, compared with ten to twenty for the move to cloud, and "if you're still charging per seat in two or three years, I think your company will struggle." The two define "outcome" differently: Sierra charges only when an agent resolves an issue or completes a sale, and Bavor's arithmetic is that resolving a case for one euro that would have cost ten euros in a call center saves nine euros each time; Zendesk co-defines outcomes with each customer, usually "resolved without human intervention" in support, and in one UK used-car marketplace case it was AI closing a GBP 500 gap to complete a sale. On scale, Zendesk expects more than 80% of interactions to be handled by AI within three years and agent-to-agent interactions to exceed 50% within two. On the people side, most Sierra customers moved people into higher-value work rather than cutting, and some added an "AI architect" role to train and tune agents<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-33">[4-33]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Change the unit of billing and the vendor's organization gets rearranged.** Eggemeier called cybersecurity the thing he worries about most as CEO, saying "offensive AI agents are currently ahead of defensive capability"; the two disagreed on who survives, with Bavor betting on companies that own authoritative data and Eggemeier offering three tests — treat AI as both existential threat and opportunity, change the business model, and send people to the front line to work alongside customers. The implication for customer operations is direct: moving from per-seat to per-outcome hands the definition of the outcome to the customer, which forces vendors to design agent permissions, auditability and fallback processes up front — **once you are paid for outcomes, a failure stops being the customer's problem.**

**The Chinese version looks more like turning brand knowledge into context.** Jisi Technology (Chinese: 集思科技) reports that it launched 38 beauty digital-human livestream rooms on JD.com on February 28, 2022, has since generated more than RMB 6 billion in cumulative GMV for clients, and has served consumers more than 30 million times over three years; it now serves over 700 customers and more than 1,000 livestream rooms, including LVMH, Estée Lauder, L'Oréal, P&G (North Asia), Nestlé and Diageo. Founder Li Shizun's methodology yields two lines worth keeping: first, "people who use AI are ten times more productive than those who don't, and people who manage AI are ten times more efficient than those who use it"; second, a brand's core asset is not visual consistency but "context" — whether the founder's understanding of the brand, category know-how and historical judgments can be sedimented into assets an AI can call on, since visual consistency is something "any team can do reasonably well." The transferable practice is assigning a dedicated agent to each sub-scenario (ingredients and usage, head-to-head comparison, store policy) to avoid the hallucination increase that comes from one agent covering an entire domain<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-34">[4-34]</a></sup> [official claims].

## 4.11 A Twelve-Month Reversal in Conversion: The Channel Flipped, the Catalog Did Not

**In one dataset, AI traffic went from the worst channel to the best.** In March 2026, traffic arriving from AI sources converted 42% better than non-AI traffic, while in March 2025 it converted 38% worse — inside twelve months the sign of the reading flipped; visitors arriving from assistants also spent 48% longer on site and viewed 13% more pages. A companion consumer survey of more than 5,000 respondents found 39% had shopped with AI, and 85% of those thought the experience had improved. The author's central claim is that "the channel flipped, the catalog did not": the lesson from OpenAI's retreat on Instant Checkout is not about checkout technology but about product data structure — agents read structured fields, and product-page copy is nearly invisible to them<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-35">[4-35]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **Keep a sense of scope about the 42%: it comes from one e-commerce traffic sample with no category or market breakdown. But the sign change alone is enough to rewrite budget allocation — AI traffic was previously down-weighted as low quality and now has to be repriced as high-intent traffic.**

**Set it next to the conversational commerce of 4.1 and a dividing line appears.** In-conversation checkout requires products, inventory, prices and policies to be callable inside the dialogue; out-of-conversation checkout, back on the merchant's own site, requires the same fields to be readable on the surface an agent crawls. The engineering work overlaps heavily; only the party collecting payment differs. This also explains why a technical guide from a payments company gets read by merchants as a due-diligence checklist — its three questions (which channel first, how to be found by agents, whom to build with) cover the whole decision from data to channel<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-36">[4-36]</a></sup> [official]. **The conversion team's first deliverable this year may well be a field table for products and policies rather than a landing page.**

## 4.12 Summary

The AI-ification of the conversion stage comes down to four things happening at once:

1. **Personalization from segments to individuals** — LLMs make "a tailored experience for every customer" possible (Netflix's 80% of viewing coming from recommendations, Taobao's RecGPT);
2. **Pricing from seats to outcomes** — customers begin paying for "completed work" (Agentforce's $1.5 billion ARR, HubSpot's pay-per-result), while the trust problem of dynamic pricing surfaces at the same time (the Delta inquiry);
3. **The shelf from webpages to conversations** — AI agents compare prices and place orders on people's behalf, and brand equity gives way to data assets (Rufus's 300 million users, the $1 trillion forecast for 2030);
4. **The funnel from experience to data** — revenue intelligence turns sales forecasting into an algorithm (Gong's $300 million ARR, Neocrm's NeoAgent 2.0).

Conversion ends in the close; what follows the close is service. The next chapter covers the most crowded — and most misunderstood — stage of the customer lifecycle: customer service.
