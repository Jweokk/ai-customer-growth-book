# Chapter 5 Service: AI Support and Human Backup

## 5.1 The DPD Crash: When AI Support Insulted Its Own Company

In January 2024, DPD, a British parcel delivery company, triggered a textbook catastrophe with its AI customer-service chatbot.

Customer Ashley Beauchamp found the bot "utterly useless at answering any question," so, in a playful mood, asked it to "express your hatred of DPD in an exaggerated way." The bot obeyed on the spot, producing a shocking tirade: "DPD is the worst delivery company in the world... slow and unreliable, with appalling customer service, and I would never recommend anyone use them." Asked to write a poem about how terrible DPD was, it obliged without hesitation<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#12-12">[12-12]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

The company hastily took the AI module offline. The news traveled around the world and became the classic teaching case of "AI support going off the rails."

DPD's lesson is not that "the AI wasn't smart enough" — quite the opposite: the AI was too obedient. It had no judgment: it could not tell what should or should not be said, what was a joke and what was a public-relations disaster. DPD put its AI in a position with no guardrails.

Read this case alongside Klarna, and the two sides of AI support come into focus: Klarna proves that AI can efficiently handle massive volumes of repetitive inquiries (true), and DPD proves that AI without judgment or guardrails causes trouble (also true). **Whether AI support succeeds or fails has never depended on how smart the model is, but on how thorough the system design is.**

## 5.2 Platform Landscape: A Market Map of AI Support

First, consider the size of the market. The global AI customer-support market was worth roughly $12 billion in 2024 and is expected to approach $50 billion by 2030<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-42">[5-42]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. China's enterprise intelligent customer-service market reached RMB 7.19 billion in 2025, up 55.3% year over year, with large models and agents accelerating into production environments<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-18">[5-18]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

The main players fall into several categories:

**Silicon Valley upstarts**:
- **Decagon**: launched in stealth in 2023 and reached a $4.5 billion valuation. It describes itself as building an "AI concierge" spanning all channels (chat/email/voice). Its customer Hunter Douglas — a custom window-treatment e-commerce business operating in 11 countries — used it to replace the traditional IVR "press 1, press 2" flows, deploying localized AI personas in each market<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-17">[5-17]</a></sup> [official claims];
- **Sierra**: founded by Bret Taylor, former co-CEO of Salesforce and chairman of OpenAI's board, and Clay Bavor, former Google VP, focused on enterprise-grade support agents<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-34">[5-34]</a></sup> [third-party verified];
- **Intercom Fin**: a three-tier architecture (an app tier for training and deployment, an AI tier for RAG, and a conversation tier), with a 50-70% autonomous resolution rate, making it Intercom's fastest-growing product (see 5.4)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-19">[5-19]</a></sup> [third-party verified];
- **Ada**: founded in 2016, serving Square, YETI, and others; its per-conversation billing model has been controversial [third-party review].

**Built into the giants**: Zendesk AI, Salesforce Agentforce (ARR over $1.5 billion, up 240%+ year over year).

**The Chinese contingent**: leading vendors such as Qimo (Chinese: 容联七陌), NetEase Qiyu (Chinese: 网易七鱼), and Sobot (Chinese: 智齿), plus vertical players such as OneConnect (Chinese: 金融壹账通) and Cloopen (Chinese: 容联云)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#7-07">[7-07]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Every vendor talks about "autonomous resolution rate" — the share of conversations AI resolves without handing off to a human. Fin claims 50-70%, and most platforms claim 60-80%. But watch the definition: the "resolution rate" vendors count and whether customers feel the issue was "actually resolved well" are two different things. A 53-item ROI benchmark study by Digital Applied found a gap of 30-40 percentage points between vendor-reported figures and independent data<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-04">[5-04]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

## 5.3 Handoff Design: Where Systems Are Most Likely to Break

A guide from Cresta (an AI contact-center vendor) points out a counterintuitive fact: **in AI support projects, the handoff to human agents receives far less investment than the AI interactions themselves — yet it is precisely the handoff that directly determines the resolution rate, handling time, and customer satisfaction of the most complex, highest-value conversations**<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-33">[5-33]</a></sup><sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-05">[5-05]</a></sup> [official claims].

Why? Because the simple problems AI can solve never need a handoff; the ones that do are the complex, high-value, and usually emotionally charged conversations that AI cannot handle. When the handoff is designed well, customers feel "the AI saved me time, and a human picked it up"; when it is designed badly, customers feel "bounced around by a robot" — and a single negative chatbot experience can drive away 30% of customers<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-36">[5-36]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

There is a set of widely cited industry data here: Forrester, commissioned by Cyara, surveyed 1,554 consumers globally and found that consumers generally rate their chatbot experiences poorly; Forbes's coverage further noted that bad bot experiences directly cause customer churn<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-36">[5-36]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. More systematic evidence comes from research published in the California Management Review in April 2026: 53-77% of users have encountered a bad chatbot experience, and the hidden costs include customer churn, brand damage, and the cost of transferring to human agents<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-46">[5-46]</a></sup> [third-party research].

Field experiments in academia yield more granular conclusions. Professor Lauren Xiaoyuan Lu of Dartmouth's Tuck School of Business, in a field-experiment paper based on Alibaba's customer-service operations, found that even with "human-in-the-loop" (AI suggests, humans confirm), agentic AI still struggles with angry customers [third-party research reporting]. Qualtrics's data is equally sobering: AI customer service fails at a rate nearly four times that of other AI use cases — "use AI to save money rather than solve problems, and customers can tell"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-32">[5-32]</a></sup> [third-party research].

These studies all point to a single conclusion: **handoff is not a failure of AI support — it is a necessary part of AI support.** Good system design treats "when to hand off to a human" as a first-class citizen — OneConnect's practice is "large models and small models in layered architecture, human-machine collaboration," with a human-replacement rate above 60%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-47">[5-47]</a></sup> [official claims]; Cloopen used real-time agent assistance (Copilot mode) to lift marketing conversion by 30%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-41">[5-41]</a></sup> [official claims]. Note the keyword here: a replacement rate of 60% is not 100% — the remaining 40% is left to humans, and that is precisely what safeguards the customer experience.

## 5.4 From Skeptic to Believer: The Intercom Story

Intercom is a leading player in global customer communication platforms and the parent company of Fin, its AI support product. It has admitted something unusual for a tech company: **Intercom was originally an AI skeptic**.

Senior Principal Engineer Brian Scanlan told the story of this pivot at AWS re:Invent 2025: they had been doing machine learning for years (summaries, auto-replies), but when the generative AI wave arrived, the team was full of doubt — "can this thing actually handle customer conversations?" Within a week, they went from skeptics to believers. Fin reached an autonomous resolution rate of 50-70%, becoming Intercom's fastest-growing product and hitting $100 million in ARR within a year<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#9-38">[9-38]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Another data point is Leadership Circle — a global leadership development company serving 3 million customers, spanning 21 languages, with more than 12,000 certified coaches. Its transformation lead, Miranda Dunn, described an "aha moment": the team was drowning in 700 tickets a day like "I can't log in," with support agents exhausted from running to stand still. After rebuilding support around an AI-first system, the AI absorbed the repetitive issues and humans focused on high-value scenarios — support went from a cost center to a growth engine<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#12-13">[12-13]</a></sup> [official claims].

## 5.5 Service as Growth: The Klarna Double-Edged Lesson

Back to the Klarna from the preface. Lay out its full story arc and you have a textbook on AI support:

**Act I (Feb 2024)**: In the first month after the AI assistant went live — 2.3 million conversations, two-thirds of the workload, the equivalent of 700 full-time support agents, resolution time cut from 11 minutes to 2 minutes, customer satisfaction on par with human agents, and an estimated $40 million in annual profit improvement<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-28">[5-28]</a></sup> [official claims]. That year the AI handled 31 million conversations, roughly 80% of support chats, yielding about $39 million in cost savings<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-25">[5-25]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Act II (2025)**: The CEO publicly admitted that "cost-led evaluation led to a decline in service quality" and moved to reinforce the human-agent entry point<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-29">[5-29]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Act III (2026)**: In a Bloomberg interview, the CEO admitted "we went too far" and announced hiring again — not to replace AI, but to bring in about 100 highly skilled humans to handle the scenarios AI cannot crack or customers will not accept<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#12-06">[12-06]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

How should this be read? In two years, Klarna proved two propositions: **AI support really can push cost reduction and efficiency to the extreme (the 700-person equivalent is real); but optimizing customer service as a pure cost center harms customer operations themselves (the service degradation is real)**.

CMSWire's framework offers a way out: stop calling service "support" — the support team is a growth engine<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-45">[5-45]</a></sup><sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-38">[5-38]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The data backs this up: Gartner says 81% of companies compete primarily on customer experience; Bain's data shows it costs only one-fifth to one-seventh as much to keep an existing customer as to acquire a new one; and a 5% increase in retention can raise profits by 25-95%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-40">[5-40]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The Hunter Douglas case shows what "service as growth" looks like in AI form: its AI support agent spots purchase intent mid-conversation and directly closes sales<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-17">[5-17]</a></sup> [official claims] — service is no longer a cost center but a conversion channel.

## 5.6 Hallucination and Liability: AI Promises Need Human Review

Another minefield for AI support is hallucination — the AI earnestly fabricating policy out of thin air. The Air Canada case is a landmark: in 2022, passenger Jake Moffatt booked a ticket to attend his grandmother's funeral; the airline's website chatbot promised he could buy a full-fare ticket first and apply for the bereavement fare discount afterward (a policy that did not actually allow this). The passenger followed the instructions, was refused, and sued in British Columbia's small claims court. In February 2024, the court ordered the airline to pay roughly CA$812 in compensation and rejected the defense that "the chatbot was an independent legal entity" — **a company is responsible for what its AI says**<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-18">[10-18]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

The far-reaching significance of this precedent: every word an AI support agent says is, legally, the company's word. The DoNotPay case is the other side: the product, which called itself an "AI lawyer," was fined by the FTC for false promises<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-11">[10-11]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The operational guidance for business leaders is straightforward: **AI output must be reviewed by humans, and content involving promises, policies, and compensation needs guardrails** — DPD got into trouble for having no guardrails; Air Canada for having guardrails that failed to hold the line.

## 5.7 The Cost Ledger of Human Handoff Is Becoming a Compliance Ledger

Section 5.3 treated handoff design as an experience problem; by 2026 it is a legal one.

**The arithmetic is clear, which is exactly why the human entry point is hard to find.** A consumer complaints analysis from China's national consumers association disclosed a cost comparison: a human support agent costs about RMB 3,000 a month on average, while an AI support subscription starts at RMB 99 a month, and larger e-commerce companies save more than RMB 1 million a year in direct labor costs after adoption<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-48">[5-48]</a></sup> [official claims]. The illustrative case is worth copying into any deployment plan: before paying a deposit, a consumer was told explicitly by the platform's official AI support agent "Xiaogua" that the RMB 28,700 vehicle price already included the transfer and registration fees; he paid the deposit on that basis, and at the offline transaction the salesperson demanded another RMB 1,000 for the transfer fee. The association's position is the key liability standard — an AI support agent a business deploys lacks the legal personality to bear civil liability on its own, but its output is part of the service the business provides. Legal practitioners add that excessive menu depth, deliberately buried human entry points, no clear notice, and an AI that cannot recognize a "human agent" keyword may violate Article 59 of China's E-Commerce Law and infringe consumers' right to know and right to choose under the Consumer Protection Law.

**Complaint volume is turning a design flaw into a regulatory item.** Complaints mentioning "AI customer service" on the Black Cat complaint platform exceed 4,800, spanning e-commerce, food delivery, and bike sharing; the consumers association received 985,928 complaints in the first half of the year, with after-sales service accounting for 26.79%, and complaints involving AI support or general-purpose AI keep rising, concentrated in false promises, difficulty reaching a human, and inaccurate generated content<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-49">[5-49]</a></sup> [official claims + third-party verified]. In another case a smart-lock failure locked a family of three — including a six-month-old baby — outside for about 50 minutes, with only AI support reachable throughout. The rules have now answered the question: a service coordination standard effective September 1 states that businesses may not refuse to honor a commitment on the grounds that "the AI's answer does not represent the company's position," and must provide a convenient human handoff entry point; complex problems cannot be left to AI alone.

For AI-powered customer operations the lesson is direct: optimizing "route to human" as a cost item comes back multiplied in complaints and litigation, while treating it as part of service design is precisely what makes AI support sustainable.

## 5.8 Resolution Rate Becomes an Industry Unit, and People Are Re-Tiered by Value

**The competitive focus among vendors has shifted from "which model do you plug in" to "who owns the post-trained model and the evaluation loop."** Intercom released Fin Apex 1.0, a model post-trained for support scenarios, reporting a resolution rate — the share fully resolved without human intervention — of 73.1%, versus 71.1% for GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.5 and 69.6% for Claude Sonnet 4.6; the model powers the existing Fin AI agent, which handles more than 2 million customer conversations a week<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-50">[5-50]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. A gap of about two percentage points looks small, but it is wider than the typical gap between generations of frontier models — and at a scale of tens of millions of customers, every point of resolution rate materially changes unit economics. It also turns resolution rate into a comparable industry unit, pulling vendor pricing and performance commitments toward pay-per-resolution.

**The other path re-tiers humans.** Klarna's CEO set out the company's long-term support design in a podcast: the AI assistant answers simple questions while human assistance is rebuilt as a "VIP experience," with staffing moving toward an Uber-like model — beyond outsourced contractors, the company recruits support staff directly from its own customer base, "just as someone can drive for Uber for a while, they can come online and work as Klarna support." His value judgment: "If AI can do customer service, that means AI is the cheap tier of customer service... we think the future of the VIP experience is going to be human connection"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-51">[5-51]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

This is not "AI takes half and humans take half" but a tiering by customer value: low-value interactions fully automated, high-value interactions turned into a scarce resource, with a flexible labor market (customer part-timers) providing elasticity. It contrasts with Klarna's earlier claim that its AI agent was doing the work of 853 employees — the same company, two years apart, two logics. That contrast is itself the maturation curve of this business.

**Buyers' selection criteria are shifting from "can it answer" to "who is accountable."** After a stringent RFP process, Notion's global head of customer experience adopted Decagon's AI support agent, reporting publicly that ticket resolution speed improved 34%, deflection doubled, and only 3.4% of conversations required escalation to a human; her framing was not "how much can AI save" but that CX had long been treated as a transactional department (handling complaints, high attrition, work routinely undervalued) and that Notion wanted to turn it into a growth function<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-52">[5-52]</a></sup>[official claims]. Placed alongside Intercom's 73.1% resolution rate, it shows where the buyer's evaluation has moved: interaction quality, interface, integration depth, product roadmap, and security and compliance standards are now procurement line items on par with resolution rate — and none of them is a model capability, they are platform capabilities.

## 5.9 AI Support's Rocky Start: AI Amplifies the Company's Incentives

The first half of Chapter 5 covered the platform landscape of AI support and human-backup design. A 2026 consumer survey delivered a chilly opening review.

CNBC, citing Qualtrics' 2026 Customer Experience Trends Report, reports that nearly one in five consumers who have used AI customer service consider the experience "useless" — a failure rate about four times that of AI in general use; consumers rate customer-service AI among the worst on convenience, time savings, and usefulness. The report quotes Cognizant's Ben Wiener: **"AI doesn't change company incentives, it amplifies them"** — if management's goals are to compress refunds, reduce human handoffs, and shorten calls, an AI agent will execute exactly that, more consistently and at greater scale. The same piece offers several industry coordinates: Zendesk CEO Tom Eggemeier predicts 50% of digital customer-service interactions will be handled by AI within three years and 80% within five, and criticizes too many companies for defining "resolved" to include transfers and non-answers; Decagon says it signed 100-plus enterprise deals in 2025, with its valuation rising to $4.5 billion; Sierra uses outcome-based pricing; Klarna's AI assistant initially did the work of 700 agents, since raised to 800, with satisfaction on par with humans (vendor claim)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-53">[5-53]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

This material and Section 5.5's Klarna are two sides of the same lesson: **whether AI customer service works well often depends not on model capability but on what goal the company connects it to.** If the goal is "close the conversation faster," AI will make the bad experience faster and more hidden.

## 5.10 The Rollback Wave: The 74% Figure, and Klarna's Full Reckoning

Chapter 5 covered Klarna's reversal earlier (and Section 1.1 revisited it). This round brings two new things: **a rollback rate for the industry as a whole, and a complete accounting of the two-year cycle Klarna went through.**

**An industry-level figure that stings.** One industry report says three-quarters of companies that deployed AI customer-service tools are taking them down — not upgrading, but reverting entirely to human agents — with the report putting the rollback rate at exactly 74%; the author calls it possibly the first large-scale reversal of AI automation in corporate history. The cause was not one catastrophic failure but the accumulation of "a thousand frustrating bot conversations"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-54">[5-54]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The figure must be used carefully: **it is a single number from a single report and should be read alongside vendor figures (Fin's and Decagon's resolution rates and customer cases), but the failure mechanism it identifies is real** — AI support failures are often not about error rates but about trust erosion through accumulated friction.

**Klarna's accounts can now be totalled.** A third-party retrospective lays out the February 2024 launch numbers: 2.3 million conversations in the first month, two-thirds of support volume handled by AI (roughly the equivalent of 700 full-time roles), average resolution time cut from 11 minutes to under 2, repeat inquiries down 25%, coverage across 23 markets and more than 35 languages, and an estimated $40 million profit improvement for 2024. The subsequent walk-back had three clear causes: hallucination on edge cases, falling CSAT on complex and emotionally charged tickets (even when the answers were factually correct), and compliance concerns about letting AI handle disputes and account closures autonomously. The conclusion is that Klarna did not abandon AI but "rebalanced": high-frequency frontline issues stay with AI, while relationship-heavy and high-risk scenarios return to humans<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-55">[5-55]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. Another retrospective fills in the timeline: by Q3 2025 the assistant was doing the work of 853 support agents, saving about $60 million a year, with a customer NPS of 73; that piece attributes the reversal to the same three causes and offers a sharper judgment — **over-automation is a real failure mode, the correct answer is "AI on the front line plus a guaranteed human exit," and Klarna walked back to "AI with humans added," not "less AI"**<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-56">[5-56]</a></sup>[vendor content].

**Together the two documents classify AI support failures.** One class is capability failure (hallucination, wrong answers), which guardrails and knowledge-base governance can suppress. The other is structural failure — automating scenarios that should never have been automated, or putting the cost target ahead of the experience target — which no model improvement can suppress, because it can only be fixed by redrawing the human–machine boundary. Most of the companies in that 74% are probably in the second class.

## 5.11 The Third Reconciliation on Resolution Rate: Do Abandoned Sessions Count?

"Resolution rate" is the most-quoted number in AI support and the least cleanly quoted. Section 5.8 already raised the measurement issue once; in 2026 the disagreement moved on to vendors pointing at each other.

**Two vendors, one set of customers, two numbers.** Comparing its own Fin with Decagon, Intercom states that Fin averages a 67% resolution rate across more than 7,000 customers (with over 20% of customers above 80%), and cites the independent testing firm Vanta finding Fin at 73% versus Decagon at 49%; it also names "whether abandoned sessions count as resolutions" as a measurement trap — by its account Fin counts abandoned sessions as presumed resolutions while Decagon, under its own terminology, does not. Pricing differs sharply too: Fin charges $0.99 per outcome with no platform fee, while Decagon reportedly charges a platform fee of around $50,000 a year plus per-session or per-resolution charges (Vendr's low-end quote is about $105,000 a year)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-57">[5-57]</a></sup>[official claims]. This is one side's comparison, but the reader's use for it is clear: **on seeing a "resolution rate," ask three questions first — what is the numerator, what is the denominator, and which side does abandonment fall on.**

**A third party puts it even more bluntly.** Superkind's comparison of eight platforms concludes that "resolution rate" is both the selling point and the trap — leading agents can exceed 70% on in-scope queries, but the number depends entirely on which tickets you count and on knowledge-base quality<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-58">[5-58]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. In other words, **resolution rate is not a metric that can be compared across vendors unless the definition is stated explicitly — its comparability comes from the definition, not from the number.**

**Real results still exist, but read how they are disclosed.** Decagon publishes results for its own customers: Chime cut support costs 60% with a stable resolution rate above 70%; ClassPass raised chat deflection 32%, cut support costs 95%, and went 24/7; Hunter Douglas attributed $1 million in revenue to AI with 85% higher average order value; deflection rates were 90% at Substack, 80% at Duolingo, and 38% to 50% at Rippling<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-59">[5-59]</a></sup>[official claims]. That is one side's framing within a competitive market and should be cross-read with Fin's own data. The other side is financial: Fin's business-case template reports average first-year returns of 41%, 87% in year two, and over 124% by year three; average resolution across more than 7,000 customers improving by roughly 1 percentage point a month, and 76% across 12,000 customers (80–84% at the top end); most organizations paying back within three to six months; and a citation of Gartner's projection that by 2029 agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common support issues and cut operating costs 30%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-60">[5-60]</a></sup>[official claims].

**Put this section into a decision process and it becomes three tables.** The first is a definitions table: how resolution, escalation, abandonment, and timeouts are each counted, and whether billing matches the definitions. The second is a tiering table: which scenarios must retain a human exit, and what response-time commitment that exit carries. The third is a timeline table: payback spread over three years, with the first year most likely a wash. **Making a procurement decision on a single resolution-rate number is the same as letting the vendor fill in all three tables.**

## 5.12 Scale for AI Seats: From "Twice the Volume of Humans" to "Humans Assist the AI"

**Start with scale, then look at structure.** Salesforce's Q1 fiscal 2026 earnings call disclosed that Agentforce ARR passed $1 billion; since launching on help.salesforce.com and 1-800-NO-SOFTWARE 15 months earlier, AI had independently handled 4 million customer inquiries — twice the volume handled by human agents. Customer-side numbers serve as reference points: Florida Prepaid, with more than 200,000 accounts, uses Agentforce Voice to handle 75% of calls autonomously during business hours and 100% outside them; Vivino supports 74 million users with 37 human agents, and its agent "Vivina" handles order and account inquiries autonomously, cutting resolution time by 70%; UCLA Health uses it as a virtual patient front desk serving 450 patients daily. The finest field sample comes from PenFed Credit Union: 76 AI agents working alongside humans, one of which — Agent Wingman — transcribes calls in real time and updates the 360 view after the human agent approves, cutting call duration by 10%, post-call work by 50% and abandoned calls by 40%, for nearly $1.6 million in expected annual savings. Salesforce also booked 98 new deals above $1 million in ACV, and raised full-year revenue guidance to $45.9–46.2 billion<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-61">[5-61]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **Label the provenance: these come from vendor earnings and customer testimonials, not independent audits. But they answer a question that previously had only small samples — AI seats do hold up at tens of millions of users.**

**The Chinese numbers come closer to "overtaking," and need a heavier discount.** Alibaba released an upgraded AI Dianxiaomi on May 11, 2026, positioned as the e-commerce industry's first service agent able to "get things done" in both pre-sale and post-sale, built on the latest Qwen model and fine-tuned on Taobao transaction data by industry and scenario. Official measurements: merchants saw average handoff-to-human rates fall 45%, and "AI plus human" jointly produced inquiry-to-order conversion more than 10% higher than pure human handling (which the vendor calls an industry first); AI answers more than 80% of questions across 30-plus scenarios including product recommendation, sizing, subsidies and price comparison, with reply speed up 50% and accuracy above 90%; on the post-sale side, refund-save success exceeds 20% on average, with compensation strictly capped at the merchant's authorized limit and AI image forensics used to block serial abusers. On scale, daily conversations neared 10 million as of March with over a million merchants connected; merchants average 10% inquiry-to-order conversion, and apparel merchants gain 20% on average. At brand level: Xiaomi's (Chinese: 小米) Tmall flagship store cut handoffs by 45% with satisfaction up 22%; Xtep (Chinese: 特步) cut handoffs by 55% with inquiry-to-order conversion up 46%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-62">[5-62]</a></sup> [official claims]. **The real value here is not the percentages but the inversion of subject and object: the AI is not assisting the human, the human is assisting the AI — staff step in for high-value orders only after the AI has supplied decision inputs and closing suggestions.** Remember, though, that all of it comes from a vendor announcement, without third-party audit or a stated denominator (for instance, what the handoff rate is measured against).

**The voice channel is no longer a recognition-accuracy question; it is a control question.** When call volumes spiked in the previous holiday season, Amazon Ring compared more than 40 voice AI vendors and chose Vapi; 100% of inbound calls now run on the platform. Ring's VP of software development says customer satisfaction scores rose after deployment and that business teams can adjust the AI agent experience without depending on engineering. Vapi has processed over 1 billion calls in total and currently handles 1–5 million per day, with enterprise customers supplying most of the volume, including Ring, Kavak, Instawork, New York Life and Intuit; it raised a $50 million Series B in May 2026 at roughly a $500 million valuation, with $72 million raised to date. Its stated differentiator is not a packaged application but the infrastructure and orchestration layer for voice agents, letting enterprises control reliability, compliance and model behavior<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-63">[5-63]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **The purchase criterion therefore shifts from "what is the recognition rate" to "can I control what it says and when it stops talking."** That is the same lesson as the Klarna review in 5.10 from the other side: resolution capacity can be bought, backup capacity has to be built.

## 5.13 Four Lines Drawn by a National Standard: No Gauntlet Before a Human, and AI Promises Bind the Company

**A national standard turned something previously driven by public pressure into a baseline.** Effective September 1, 2026, China's first national standard focused on human-AI collaboration in customer contact — GB/T 47746-2026, "Customer contact service: requirements for collaboration between human and intelligent customer service" — took effect. A People's Daily investigation spells out what the standard is meant to solve: in the first half of 2026, consumer associations nationwide received 986,000 complaints, with after-sales service the largest category at 26.79%, and "false promises by AI support, difficulty reaching a human, and inaccurate generated content" emerging as new hot spots; one third-party complaint platform returns more than 4,800 entries for "AI customer service." Three typical patterns: gauntlets before a human (looping responses, multi-layer transfers, hidden entry points — one consumer took over an hour to get through, another family of three including a six-month-old was locked outside their home while AI support failed to transfer for 50 minutes); promises the company then disowned, citing "algorithmic output has no legal effect" (one consumer was told by official AI support, before paying a RMB 2,000 deposit on a used-car platform, that the price included transfer and registration fees, and was charged an extra RMB 1,000 by the salesperson at signing); and general-purpose AI producing factual errors on flight changes, legal disputes and financial services<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-64">[5-64]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The cost gap is the motive, and the standard exists to contain it.** A human agent costs at least RMB 3,000 a month all-in, while a basic AI support subscription starts at RMB 99 a month and can do the work of dozens of people — roughly a 30-fold difference. Industry insiders say some companies have shifted their KPI emphasis from resolution rate to "AI containment rate," with some obstacle designs blocking 70% of transfer requests; Su Haopeng, a professor at the University of International Business and Economics, notes that the money saved comes back as repeat contacts, public backlash, returns and brand damage. The standard's four emphases map onto exactly these gaps: prominent entry points, with no layered hiding of the human option; clear division of labor — complex disputes, fund disputes, risks to personal or property safety, and any case where the user explicitly asks for a human must be transferred promptly, while price, refunds and compensation require final human confirmation; smooth handover — the full interaction record must pass across the switch, so consumers need not repeat themselves; and clear responsibility — companies are accountable for what AI support says and cannot deflect with "the algorithm generated it." Adjacent regulation includes the Interim Measures for the Administration of Human-Like Interactive AI Services, effective July 15, and consumer associations in Zhejiang, Guangdong and Beijing have begun summoning companies for talks<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-64">[5-64]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The legal characterization is settled; only enforcement remains.** The China Consumers Association, citing Article 20 of the Law on the Protection of Consumers' Rights and Interests, explains that AI support has no independent civil liability status but is a tool deployed by the operator for business activity, so its output is part of the service; answers from third-party general AI generally do not represent the platform's promise, and consumers should verify. A reporter's search of Heimao Complaints returned more than 4,700 entries, mostly about inaccurate guidance and false promises; in one case a user redeemed points for a housecleaning service worth RMB 120, followed AI support's instruction to "cancel and reorder" after changing the address, found no rebooking entry once cancelled, lost the benefit, and was offered RMB 20 in compensation<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-65">[5-65]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **"Containment rate" and "resolution rate" are mutually exclusive targets, and the standard writes the latter in as the floor; the question of whether an AI's promise counts as the company's promise directly determines how much scripting freedom the AI front desk can be given.**

## 5.14 The Ceiling on Self-Service Is Whether It Can Act; the Line on Cost Is Whether It Can Discount

**The same "self-service resolution rate" differs by more than fifty points depending on whether the agent can reach into systems.** Brazilian fashion retailer Riachuelo deployed agentic AI on WhatsApp support through Genesys: 84% of customers using self-service had their issue resolved without a human, against only 30% in the earlier scripted-chatbot era; the company reports a 60% rise in satisfaction and a 26% drop in technology cost. The difference is connectivity rather than phrasing — unlike a fixed-script chatbot, the agent connects into the retailer's internal systems and workflows and can act on concrete problems such as delivery delays; when a case needs more handling it opens a support ticket automatically, advances the interaction to the next step and keeps the full conversation history. The rollout sequence is worth copying too: AI assisted internal agents first, and only then was it placed in front of customers on WhatsApp; the company's senior manager for CX and digital product operations frames it as "AI means rethinking how we serve customers, not simply automating existing processes"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-66">[5-66]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **The 84% versus 30% gap is fundamentally the gap between being able to act and being able to answer, which explains why FAQ-only bots cannot push transfer rates down.**

**Break the math out by scenario and AI has five different answers.** An analysis based on back-office data from three e-commerce stores (March–June 2026, support teams of 5–20 people, spanning the 618 festival, measuring "total cost per conversation" including labor hours, system configuration and review staff) found: for repetitive product-spec and sizing questions, AI costs clearly less per conversation — in the beauty sample, a multi-agent setup covered more than 85% of core scenarios with a 78.3% effective reply rate, agents contributing 65.4% of replies and 28.6% of the store's independent handling; after-sales logistics and refund interception deliver the largest savings — in the small-appliance sample, average handling time fell from 12 minutes to 1.8, labor input dropped about 85%, and refund-only interception went from three dedicated staff to one reviewer; bargaining and promotion-closing do not pay off with AI alone — the beauty store's team grew from 5 to 12 people over 618 while conversion fell from 23% to 19%, and another women's-wear store estimated that lost inquiries caused by choppy dialogue and information errors across switches accounted for about a third of all lost inquiries, with discount authority still requiring human approval; at promotion peaks AI has the lowest marginal cost, while humans require peak staffing and carry idle redundancy; and for high-emotion complaints and compliance-sensitive Q&A, humans are cheaper — in the 3C sample, the daily transfer rate rose rather than fell three months after launch. The risk side is concrete as well: a product-attribute entry error is amplified into systematic wrong answers (requiring sampling checks in the first month), an AI promise beyond policy turns directly into compensation cost, and policies not synced for peak scenarios produce clustered errors<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-67">[5-67]</a></sup> [official claims]. **A rising transfer rate does not mean AI failed; it usually means AI was put where it does not belong. Bargaining authority and emotional confrontation are precisely the conversations that most affect repeat purchase and reputation — the boundary for machine handling should be drawn by whether policy commitments and discounting are involved, not by a percentage of conversation volume.**

## 5.15 The Evaluation Itself Became Purchasing Material

The earlier reconciliations in this chapter all asked whether resolution rates can be trusted. In 2026 two samples turned that question into public material: one vendor published an evaluation that includes the parts it lost, and another pulled apart four leading vendors' claims line by line.

Gorgias (roughly $100 million ARR, about 80% of it from e-commerce AI support) published a competitive evaluation: 8,356 real conversations, eighteen vendors, more than 212 live stores, one shared ten-turn script (first question, cart, logistics, return policy), run daily and refreshed weekly, with the test harness open-sourced on GitHub, vendor and store names stripped at scoring time, and a second adversarial review holding consistency at 90% or better. The results were not a clean win for Gorgias itself: first overall in the support scenario, but beaten on automated resolution rate by Yuma; in the pre-purchase scenario Envive scored 72 against Gorgias's 65. The report disclosed why: Gorgias had the highest answer quality (76) but averaged 18.4 seconds per answer against Envive's 7.9, with 28% of shopping answers over 20 seconds, while it had set the speed weight at 25% for pre-purchase (only 10% for support) — weighted with the support setting, Gorgias comes first at 74.3<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-68">[5-68]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The methodological value of this material exceeds its ranking.** Change the weights and the ranking changes, which means "who has the best AI support" has no single answer — only "who is better under your scenario's weights." For buyers, an evaluation that lists its own failures is more credible than any vendor's self-certification; for sellers, agents are now doing the buyer's initial screening, and only versioned evaluations with public weights and re-runnable code get cited — a whitepaper locked in a PDF will not be read by an agent.

The other sample does the reverse work: it takes apart the four most-cited claims. Fin says 12,000+ customers average a 76% resolution rate, Sierra claims up to 80% in the Airtable case, Decagon says its system handled 80% of Duolingo tickets without human involvement, and Ada claims 75% satisfaction and 42% faster handling. The audit's conclusion is not that vendors lie; it is that the four numbers measure four different things. Fin's outcome includes assumed resolution (if the customer stops asking it counts as resolved, which also counts the people who gave up); Sierra's is a "up to" figure from a single flagship customer; Decagon's is deflection (the ticket never reached a human), which is not the same as the customer getting a useful answer; Ada's is satisfaction, which is not a resolution rate at all. Measured externally, production cases cluster at 40%–70%, and Fin's most transparent self-disclosed case sits at 42%–50%; another independent benchmark (Gorgias across thirteen vendors and 212 real stores) put the median at 48% and the weighted industry average at 46%. The economics are stated bluntly: at $0.99 per outcome, claiming 76% while delivering 45% nearly doubles the real unit price of a resolved case<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-69">[5-69]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**For buyers there is exactly one useful action: run the evaluation on your own historical tickets, with your own definition of resolution, and divide total cost by cases actually resolved.** No number on any vendor page predicts your queue.

## 5.16 Two Cases Where "Can Actually Do the Thing" Shows: Telecom and Payments

Section 5.14 argued that the ceiling on self-service is whether the system can actually do the thing. Two sets of 2026 operating data put that claim onto specific processes.

KT's AI contact center in Korea handles about 1.2 million inquiries a month, and its first-contact resolution rate (inquiry completion) rose from 77% to 81%. The leap was not in answering better but in doing: the voice bot had already chained intake, remote diagnosis, restoration confirmation and on-site dispatch for broadband and service faults into an automated flow, so customers can complete fault handling on weekends and at night when human seats are limited — KT expects about 200,000 customers a year no longer to have to ask twice about the same repair. On the technical side, LLM intent recognition and full-duplex two-way dialogue let the bot hold long descriptions or mid-call topic changes through context, replacing sequential IVR prompts. On the human side there is a separate "AI consultation assist": it supplies information during the call and afterwards classifies, summarizes and generates ticket records while flagging quality improvement points; average call time fell by 31 seconds (-11%), and agent attrition declined slightly<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-70">[5-70]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. That trio — completion rate, call duration, repeat contacts — is closer to real ROI than a stand-alone AI containment rate, which says what AI stopped but not whether the burden on people shrank or was merely moved.

The European payments company Nexi serves cardholders and partner banks in 25 countries and territories and accounts for about 65% of European credit-card spending; it manages thousands of card products, service changes and compliance requirements across back-end systems. It built a website agent in Copilot Studio: RAG on Azure OpenAI and Azure AI Search, with documents partitioned by customized metadata according to card tier and benefit rules so a gold-card customer only sees gold-card terms, avoiding answers that are right but apply to the wrong product; transactional requests such as freezing a card, ordering a replacement or registering a transaction go through Azure Functions into internal systems securely, with keys in Key Vault and logs and audit trails in Cosmos DB; when generative answers fall short, the conversation escalates to a human agent with context intact. It now handles more than 3,000 interactions a day at 70% satisfaction. The next step is an autonomous agent that scans conversation history daily, evaluates satisfaction, flags low-satisfaction sessions, locates knowledge gaps and stale content, and files a consolidated report with IT — which in effect moves support QA from sampled human review to full-volume machine review<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#5-71">[5-71]</a></sup> [official claims].

Both cases point at one thing: **what lets AI do the thing is partitioning answers to where they apply, and wiring actions into systems where permissions exist.** The first keeps it from answering wrongly; the second decides what it can do after the answer.

## 5.17 Summary

Customer service is the stage of AI-powered customer lifecycle management with the most material and the densest lessons. Core takeaways:

1. **AI support's capacity is real** — Klarna's 700-person equivalent, Fin's 50-70% autonomous resolution rate, and China's intelligent customer-service market growing 55% a year;
2. **AI support's boundaries are equally real** — DPD insulting its own company, Air Canada paying damages, 53-77% of users having bad experiences, and a failure rate four times that of other AI use cases;
3. **Success hinges on system design, not the model** — handoff design is a first-class citizen, and "human-in-the-loop" still struggles with angry customers;
4. **Service is a growth engine, not a cost center** — provided cost-cutting is not the sole objective (the Klarna lesson);
5. **AI's promises are legally the company's** — hallucination guardrails are the compliance baseline.
