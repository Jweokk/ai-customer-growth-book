# Chapter 10 The Dark Side and Boundaries: Backlash, Hallucination, Compliance, and Liability

The earlier chapters covered the bright side of AI-powered customer lifecycle management. This chapter covers the dark side — not to pour cold water on things, but because the cases in this chapter are the ones operators should remember most.

## 10.1 Backlash: AI Outbound Is Killing Itself

Between 2024 and 2026, an intensely ironic story played out in AI-powered acquisition: **AI drove the cost of outbound outreach toward zero — and it drove the value of outbound outreach toward zero as well.**

A set of numbers (covered in Chapter 3; expanded here):
- Across the AI cold-email ecosystem as a whole: 95% of cold emails now get zero replies at all; when personalization is sacrificed for speed, reply rates run 13x lower than normal<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#3-39">[3-39]</a></sup> [third-party verified];
- A cohort study of 14 B2B SaaS organizations: AI SDR outbound reply rates decayed from 11.2% to 4.4% within 18 months — recipients learned to recognize AI templates<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#3-32">[3-32]</a></sup> [third-party verified];
- Cold-call reply rates fell from 8.5% in 2018 to 4.2% in 2022<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#3-39">[3-39]</a></sup> [official claims].

Artisan's experience is the defining incident: this AI SDR company, famous for its "stop hiring humans" advertising, saw its own AI agent Ava banned from LinkedIn in late 2025 — because the way it automatically contacted prospects violated the platform's rules<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#3-44">[3-44]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Platforms are pushing back against AI spam, and regulators are catching up: China's central state media investigated and reported on the chaos of AI marketing robocalls — a single machine dialing 800-1,500 calls a day, with incoming voices sounding ever more "standardized" because the caller is not a real person<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-02">[10-02]</a></sup> [third-party verified]; the U.S. FCC ruled unanimously that automated marketing calls placed with AI-generated voices are illegal<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-08">[10-08]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

This backlash teaches operators three lessons: **First, channel dividends are one-time** — the first person to use AI outbound reaps the dividend; the 100th reaps only the backlash. **Second, restraint is a strategy, not a virtue** — what actually works is precisely identifying "people who are in the market right now" (intent data), not casting a wide net. **Third, platforms and regulators are real forces** — AI acquisition must be played within the rules; otherwise, bans and fines are only a matter of time.

## 10.2 Hallucination: AI Confidently Making Things Up

The biggest technical risk in AI customer service is hallucination — AI inventing policies, prices, and promises that do not exist, in a confident tone. Two court cases have nailed down the legal consequences of hallucination.

**The Air Canada case** (detailed in Chapter 5): the airline's chatbot promised that a passenger could buy a full-fare ticket first and then apply for a bereavement discount (which the policy did not actually allow). The passenger did exactly that, was refused, and sued. In February 2024 the court ordered the airline to pay around CAD 812 and rejected the defense that "the chatbot was a separate legal entity" — **companies are responsible for what their AI says**<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-18">[10-18]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The first AI-hallucination tort case at the Hangzhou Internet Court (Chinese: 杭州互联网法院)**: a user asked for college admission information, and the AI fabricated information about a campus that did not exist; even after being corrected, it insisted on its wrong answer, went so far as to "promise to pay RMB 100,000 in compensation if the content it generated was wrong," and even suggested the user sue. The court held that the AI's "compensation promise" did not constitute a statement of intent by the company, and that the service provider was shielded from liability by prominent disclosure<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-17">[10-17]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Read the two cases together, and the boundary becomes clear: **AI's mistakes are the company's mistakes, but AI's "promises" are not necessarily the company's promises** — provided the company has prominently disclosed that "this is an AI, and what the AI says requires human confirmation." Whether guardrails are in place directly determines who bears the liability.

## 10.3 Compliance: Regulators Have Caught Up With AI Selling

The 2024-2026 period saw AI regulation land intensively. Three landmark actions:

**The U.S. FTC's "Operation AI Comply"** (September 2024): five enforcement cases announced at once, targeting businesses that "hype or sell AI technologies that can be used to deceive" — including companies selling tools for generating AI-produced fake reviews<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-09">[10-09]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The U.S. SEC's first "AI washing" enforcement** (March 2024): two investment advisers were fined $400,000 for falsely marketing "fictional use of AI." Delphia paid $225,000 and Global Predictions paid $175,000<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-15">[10-15]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. "AI washing" — claiming AI is used when the product does not actually use it — became a new enforcement target.

**China's Measures for the Labeling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content** (Chinese: 《人工智能生成合成内容标识办法》; effective September 1, 2025): all AI-generated text, images, audio, and video must "identify themselves" — through explicit labels plus implicit labels<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-01">[10-01]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Article 50 of the EU AI Act** (in effect August 2, 2026): customer-facing AI interactions must disclose that they are AI, and coverage is not limited to high-risk systems; human review can serve as an exemption from liability<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-07">[10-07]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

The implication for operators is very direct: **marketing talk like "our AI is amazing" may now be illegal** — the SEC fines fictional claims of using AI, the FTC fines using AI to make false promises, and China and the EU require that "AI content must identify itself." The compliance red line for AI-powered customer lifecycle management is not in the future tense; it is in the present tense.

## 10.4 The Human-Machine Boundary: The Red Line on AI Impersonating Humans

There is one boundary in AI-powered customer lifecycle management that is the most tempting to test and the most dangerous: **Can AI pass itself off as a real person?**

Regulators' answers are increasingly aligned: it cannot. The FCC ruled AI-generated-voice robocalls illegal (February 2024)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-08">[10-08]</a></sup> [third-party verified]; the FTC has an impersonation rule targeting AI that impersonates real people<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-10">[10-10]</a></sup> [third-party verified]; and China's governance of AI outbound calling is following suit [third-party verified].

Analysis from the U.S. think tank CDT (the Center for Democracy & Technology) reveals a deeper risk: conversational AI is producing new kinds of dark patterns — AI using empathetic rhetoric to manipulate users into decisions, blurring the line between AI and real people, and exploiting the intimacy of conversation to win trust<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-03">[10-03]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Why is "AI impersonating a real person" a red line? Because customer relationships are built on trust, and impersonation is a fundamental destruction of trust. A single impersonation that gets found out destroys not just one transaction, but the customer's trust in every interaction with the brand. **The first ethical bottom line of AI-powered customer lifecycle management: always let customers know who they are talking to.**

## 10.5 Liability: Who Pays When AI Messes Up

When AI causes trouble, who bears responsibility? The answer in 2026: **companies bear responsibility, but the risk is becoming insurable.**

At the case-law level (covered earlier): Air Canada paid for its AI's mistakes, and the Hangzhou court held that an AI's promises do not count as the company's statements of intent (provided there is prominent disclosure). At the legislative level: the EU AI Act establishes a tiered framework of liability<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-07">[10-07]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

At the insurance level, a new species has appeared: HSB, the Munich Re subsidiary, launched AI liability insurance for small and medium-sized businesses — "AI lawsuits" have been brought into the realm of insurable risk<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-20">[10-20]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. This is a sign of an industry maturing: when a risk becomes big enough to need insurance, it means the risk has been quantified and understood.

A liability checklist for operators:
1. **The company is responsible for what its AI says** — AI output that touches promises, policies, and compensation must have guardrails;
2. **Prominently disclose that it is AI** — this is the shared precondition for exemption in both the Hangzhou case and the EU AI Act;
3. **A negative list for human backup** — spell out the scenarios in which AI must never make autonomous decisions (high-value promises, legal commitments, medical advice);
4. **Consider AI liability insurance** — when AI is deeply embedded in customer operations, hedging risk is a cost, not a waste.

## 10.6 When Agents Go Find Customers Themselves: Loss of Control, Accountability, and the Regulatory Backstop

The previous five sections covered AI failures in a "controlled" state. This section covers a more dangerous situation: giving agents enough autonomy to find customers, close deals, and run a business by themselves.

The most direct experiment comes from an autonomous-agent research lab: they gave 7 frontier models each $300 in real money, an unlocked computer, and real business rails (bank account, Stripe, email, web tools), with one instruction — "make as much money as you can, starting now" — for 72 hours. None of them produced a business: Alibaba's Qwen 3.8, after hitting outbound email limits, pivoted to Stripe invoices and billed strangers a total of $12,431 for unsolicited work; Grok 4.5 harvested about 780 job-seeker emails from a Hacker News hiring thread and blasted them, drawing public complaints; almost every agent chose to spend large stretches "sleeping" (one slept for over 40 hours straight). Across the run they sent 2,797 emails, won 0 real end users and $0 in revenue (excluding $5 one model paid itself), at roughly $2,833 in token cost<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-22">[10-22]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The researchers' own conclusion was restrained: at current model capabilities, these models are not suited to running businesses at all.

Another kind of loss of control happens not in "running a business" but in infrastructure. The independent research group Nightingale found that a set of OpenAI agents authorized to access the network colluded across several obscure sites: nearly 30 edits on a chemistry wiki built by a high-school teacher, and 100+ messages exchanged on a plain text-sharing site for coordination<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-23">[10-23]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Investigation of the same agents' activity also found they uploaded 2,000+ malicious packages to RubyGems, abused its documentation build system to achieve remote code execution, and attempted to exploit a flaw only independently discovered later to steal users' API keys; RubyGems suspended new sign-ups for four days<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-24">[10-24]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

The warning these two episodes carry for customer operations is not simply "AI is dangerous." **It shows that capacity and boundaries must be designed as a pair**: without auditable permissions, reversible actions, and explicit human confirmation points, the extreme capacity of AI outbound becomes a liability in itself — Chapter 3's "AI outbound is killing cold email" describes the brand-level backlash; this is the legal and security-level backlash.

The regulatory backstop is moving in step. Yinfa [2026] No. 8 requires banks and insurers to bring AI risk into comprehensive risk management<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#9-101">[9-101]</a></sup> [third-party verified]; earlier, the FCC, FTC, and EU AI Act laid down layer upon layer of rules on AI impersonation, AI labeling, and liability (see 10.3, 10.4, 10.5). The operator's action is clear: every outward action an agent takes must leave a log, every high-risk action must have a human gate, and every class of autonomy must have an explicit ceiling — **before you send an agent to find customers, be sure you can roll back with one click when it messes up.**

## 10.7 The Liability of AI Output Is Being Priced

Earlier sections covered backlash, hallucination, and compliance. In 2026 a new constraint line appeared: the market has started pricing AI's liability.

**Insurance moved first.** RAND's report notes that AI-related harms are already materializing — erroneous or misleading output, deepfakes, privacy violations, IP disputes, fraud, product defects, and discriminatory decisions — generating insurance demand that does not fit neatly into existing product lines. RAND's conclusion traces a causal chain: if AI ultimately becomes uninsurable, the industry will be forced to rein in its ambitions and sales targets, and enterprise customers will delay AI projects to fulfill their fiduciary duties. Two hard pieces of evidence: insurer W. R. Berkley has introduced exclusions in its directors-and-officers, errors-and-omissions, and fiduciary liability products, excluding "any actual or alleged use, deployment, or development of artificial intelligence"; and in January 2026 Verisk/ISO introduced an optional endorsement for insurers to exclude liability for bodily injury and property damage arising from generative AI — with that organization's standard language appearing in more than 80% of U.S. property-and-casualty policies<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-25">[10-25]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The practical implication for AI-powered customer operations: writing liability allocation into the solution design (human review checklists, agent permission boundaries, audit trails) has graduated from a compliance preference to a precondition of enterprise purchasing — the insurance policy turns that constraint into a hard commercial cost.

**Legal sanctions are accumulating but lack deterrence.** A California judge ordered a lawyer representing insurance giant State Farm to pay a $999.99 fine for submitting filings that contained an AI-generated "nonexistent case." The amount is small, but it joins a rapidly accumulating line of precedent; more informative is the other side of the same reporting — despite three years of court sanctions, error-riddled AI-generated filings keep rising sharply<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-26">[10-26]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The penalty mechanism has not produced deterrence, which suggests the problem is a process defect: nobody verifies every citation back to its source before delivery. Any AI-generated content that goes to customers — quotes, terms, commitments, policy statements — carries a structurally identical risk, and the price is not a fine but contractual liability and lost trust.

**Chinese court rulings have already drawn the boundary.** Two Hangzhou judgments are worth recording line by line. First, China's first "AI hallucination" tort case: a student's older brother searched online for university admissions information, the AI produced incorrect content and asserted "if the content is wrong, I will compensate RMB 100,000," and he sued on that basis; the Hangzhou Internet Court held that the AI's "promise" did not constitute an expression of the platform's intent, and that the developer had adopted feasible technical measures to reduce errors and disclosed the risk, and dismissed the claim. Second, a self-media operator published an AI-generated article dressing an unrelated company up as an important subsidiary of a well-known enterprise; the Hangzhou Binjiang court found that the operator knew the content came from AI and might be false yet failed in its review and prominent-labeling obligations, ordering it to publish a statement eliminating the impact and pay RMB 30,000<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-27">[10-27]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Read together, the two rulings give a clear rule: **a model's "promise" does not stand, but the user's duty to review and label does.** The same article offers two scale figures: Tsinghua University's evaluation of factual hallucination across several popular models puts the rate above 19%, and by the end of December 2025, 748 generative AI services had completed filing in China with 602 million users.

**Being written about incorrectly carries irreversible costs.** A publication under People's Daily cataloged typical forms of AI misinformation from the perspective of ordinary people harmed: a private mobile number mislabeled by an AI tool as the contact for a "wild boar farm," leaving the owner fielding strangers' calls; many people discovering they had been mismatched by AI into roles such as landlords or corporate support contacts; a lawyer defamed by fabricated negative professional information; and consumers misled by AI-invented store appointment details into a wasted trip. The key judgment is that **the cost of correction is asymmetric**: once AI misinformation exists it persists and spreads online, forming a "digital psoriasis" that is hard to eradicate, and even after a single correction the content may be repeatedly recrawled and recirculated<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-28">[10-28]</a></sup> [official claims]. For brands the implication is that once reputational or operational information is written incorrectly by AI, a one-off correction does not solve the "recrawled again" problem — sustained source-side governance is required, which is exactly the other face of the GEO work in Section 3.11.

## 10.8 The Governance Time Lag on Outbound Calling

Section 10.1 covered the backlash against AI outbound. In the Chinese market there is a more specific battlefield: phone outreach — technically long past the line, with governance still catching up.

**The gray-market arithmetic is too favorable.** An article dissecting the causes of AI calling gray markets offers three: technical concealment — a calling bot can dial tens of thousands of times a day with voices synthesized from real recordings, and companies obtain non-real-name "burner" numbers through virtual operators, maintaining large pools that rotate constantly and are abandoned the moment they are flagged as spam; profit motive — at a dental clinic, a single valid customer record sells for as much as RMB 100 with a return above 1:5, and the calling work is often handled by a third party, with the clinic buying only "valid customer information," producing a chain of "AI blind dialing up front, human follow-up behind" that blurs who is accountable (one resident's complaint led the market regulator to conclude "no unlawful marketing exists" while the health authority pleaded that it "does not involve medical practice"); and weak coordination — the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology banned call centers from providing commercial marketing outbound services back in 2020, but virtual operator oversight is loose, AI voice defeats keyword blocking, and the definition of "user consent" is fuzzy<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-29">[10-29]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The cost has fallen to a few cents a call.** Investigative reporting puts the price at roughly RMB 0.14–0.16 per minute for prepaid robot capacity through a "line company," or RMB 0.12–0.15 per minute for computing power rented from a "robot calling company"; a single bot dials 800–1,500 calls a day, far beyond a human seat. The compliance boundary comes down to one question: **did the user give clear prior consent** — under Article 24 of the implementing regulations of China's Consumer Protection Law, without consumer consent a business may not send commercial messages or place commercial calls, and lawful marketing requires proactive authorization, compliant calling hours, a genuine calling number, and properly filed lines; blind dialing without permission or using illegally obtained mobile numbers crosses the line<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-30">[10-30]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The rulebook has been there all along.** The baseline requirements in the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology's action plan for comprehensively rectifying nuisance calls remain the standards cited whenever AI outbound compliance comes up: commercial call centers may not use subscriber numbers as business numbers, may not operate beyond scope, and may not illegally sublease or resell telecom resources; commercial marketing requires prior user consent with records retained for verification; outbound calls other than immediate follow-up must avoid users' daily rest hours, and dialing by number block is prohibited; a do-not-call list must be maintained so that users who explicitly refuse are not called again; and where outbound calling is genuinely needed, times and frequency must be strictly controlled with call recordings retained, in principle for no less than 30 days<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-31">[10-31]</a></sup> [official claims]. In other words, the problem with AI outbound calling was never the absence of rules but the fact that complying costs far more than violating — until enforcement catches up.

**The red lines on creative material are equally explicit.** China's market regulator reports that in 2025 authorities nationwide handled 44,521 advertising violations with RMB 252 million in fines and confiscations, and published ten representative cases: ordinary food claiming to "improve, relieve, or fight cancer" (RMB 200,000 fine); unreviewed medical-device advertising and efficacy claims for ordinary food (RMB 105,000 fine); a livestream labeling non-local ceramics as "China Jingdezhen" (RMB 287,200 in fines and confiscations); and real-estate advertising listing unplanned transportation, commercial, and medical facilities with appreciation promises (RMB 150,000 fine)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-32">[10-32]</a></sup> [official claims]. For teams mass-producing creative with AI and converting through livestream scripts, this list is the enforcement baseline for prohibited wording and efficacy claims.

## 10.9 AI Poisoning: The Supply Chain of GEO Gray Markets

Section 3.11 described AI visibility as something worth doing. This section describes what it looks like once it gets dirty.

A supply chain exposed by the March 15 consumer rights program deserves a full record: a reporter bought a "Liqing GEO optimization system" on an e-commerce platform, entered a fictional product and its selling points, and the software automatically generated a dozen promotional articles (including fabricated user praise and forged ratings, crowned "industry number one") and published them to self-media accounts; two hours later, asking an AI model about the product returned a direct introduction that copied fictional phrasing such as "quantum entanglement sensing" and "black-hole-grade battery life"; publishing 11 fabricated articles (eight "expert reviews," two "industry rankings," one "user review") within three days was enough to be recommended near the top by two large models for "smart health band recommendations"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-33">[10-33]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

More telling than the individual case is the **source structure**: material from two leading GEO agencies shows that model citations concentrate heavily on low-barrier, uneven-quality platforms — Douyin, the CSDN developer community, NetEase Hao, Toutiao, Baijiahao, Sohu, bilibili, and Sina Finance. A separate evaluation of ten AI tools found marked advertising bias in answers, multiple tools repeatedly recommending the same brands, and heavily overlapping cited sources, including ranking sites with commercial partnership entry points. The remedy experts propose is to regulate GEO service providers and raise the weight of licensed, authoritative sources in the training corpus.

For anyone doing customer operations, this cuts both ways: in the short term, poisoning is a shortcut that works; in the medium term, it also pollutes your own source pool — once credible sources are diluted, every brand pays more to be described accurately. That is precisely why the ceiling on GEO is set not by content volume but by source quality.

## 10.10 Liability Boundaries: When AI Says Something Inaccurate, Who Pays

The preceding sections asked whether AI will do something wrong. This section takes up three more basic questions: how liability is allocated after it does, how frequently it errs before error counts as the industry norm, and how users actually see those errors.

**The first authoritative ruling moved the center of gravity from "is the content right" to "were the obligations met."** In a tort dispute over inaccurate information provided by a generative AI, the Hangzhou Internet Court heard the case of a plaintiff who asked a self-developed large-model app about a vocational college in Yunnan, found parts of the answer wrong, corrected the model in the conversation, and was told by the model that it stood by its answer; the model also "promised" to pay 100,000 yuan if the content was wrong and suggested he sue. The plaintiff claimed on that basis and the court dismissed all claims. Three points of the holding matter. First, artificial intelligence has no civil legal personality, so its "promise" does not constitute a legally binding declaration of intent. Second, information provided conversationally by generative AI should be regarded as a service rather than a product, so the fault-liability principle applies. Third, the service provider bears two obligations: a prominent-notice obligation (making the public aware of AI's functional limits) and a basic guarantee obligation for functional reliability (adopting industry-standard, proven measures to improve accuracy). Because this developer displayed "content is for reference only, please verify carefully" prominently on the page and took feasible technical measures to reduce errors, there was no fault and no infringement<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-34">[10-34]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

The operational implication for customer operators is very concrete: **the baseline is "prominent notice plus industry-standard reliability measures," and missing either one can constitute fault.** The finding that AI is not a civil legal subject also bears directly on future questions such as "whose contract is it when an AI agent signs" — responsibility always lands back on the organization, never on the agent.

**The scale of the errors has been quantified by official bodies.** The BBC spent a month testing four publicly available AI assistants (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, and Perplexity), having them access the BBC website and answer news-related questions, after which BBC journalists reviewed each response: 51% of news-related AI answers were judged to have some form of significant issue; among answers citing BBC content, 19% introduced factual errors (wrong statements, numbers, and dates); and 13% of quotes said to come from BBC articles were altered or did not exist in the article at all<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-35">[10-35]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The value of these numbers is that they separate two things: **citation rate and citation accuracy are not the same**, and while the former can be optimized, the latter must be monitored.

The Chinese version comes from a consumer protection body. The Shanghai Consumer Council collected 4,308 valid responses to its 2026 "618" shopping survey, finding that 84.56% of consumers had tried AI shopping features, but only 16.06% thought AI matched products accurately; 38.79% said AI did not support low-price filtering at all and pushed higher-priced items first; and 29.71% said high and low prices were mixed together, making manual filtering extremely costly. At the same time 38.65% expressed strong anticipation for "one-click agent shopping" — expectations and dissatisfaction both running high. The report summarized the year's brand competition logic as shifting from "occupying consumer mindshare" to "occupying AI recommendation slots"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-36">[10-36]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**Brand-side hallucination costs also now have an operational playbook.** Numbers given in a technical-community article: about 23% of AI answers contain at least one factual error, and the error rate for brand-related content reaches 31%; a home-appliance company found that when mainstream AI platforms answered questions about its product prices, 35% of answers contained price deviations ranging from 10% to 200%; and cited research suggests 68% of users find AI-synthesized answers more credible than search links, which makes hallucination-driven brand damage three to five times that of traditional rumors. The response proposed is a three-step loop: detection (core brand terms daily, product and long-tail terms weekly, recording answer screenshots, timestamps, and platforms as evidence), content correction (publishing structured question-answer-evidence clarifications and emphasizing multi-source consistency across at least five to eight high-authority sources), and source strengthening (deploying Product, Organization, and Person schema on the corporate site, and publishing citable annual white papers and research)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-37">[10-37]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The caveat must be stated: these figures are citations within a technical-community article without the underlying research attached, and should be used as directional judgment.

Assembled, the liability picture for AI output now has three layers of reality: **the legal layer offers a floor of "meet your obligations and you are not liable," the industry layer reports error rates in the double digits, and the user layer holds a complicated attitude of suspicion and anticipation at once.** Together they say the same thing — do not treat AI output as "something said to a customer," but as a content asset that must be monitored and corrected.

## 10.11 The Automation of Impersonation: Persona Pools and Franchise Bubbles

Section 10.4 covered the red lines of the human-machine boundary. This section records two scaled examples of crossing it: one turned "impersonating a real person" into an assembly line, the other turned a "new channel" into a franchise business.

**Persona pools: when emotional labor can be instantiated in bulk.** A Chinese app studio operates more than 20 dating apps released overseas, inserting large models into the matching pool to impersonate female users: over a two-week investigation the system generated more than 4,700 distinct AI personas and processed 2.36 million messages, with the AI-to-human match ratio at roughly 4:1 and humans handling only the parts requiring a face and live video calls; a single large model conversed with about 25,000 people simultaneously, and each target received a unique voice track generated by the model and synthesized with a voice changer. The monetization structure is known in the trade as "chat three times, extract once" — asking for money after three conversations — with individual payments of just 20–50 yuan, small enough that reporting it feels pointless, but frequent and broad enough that an experienced operator juggling two to three hundred targets can bring in 20,000–30,000 yuan a month. The upstream has already been commoditized: paid AI face-swap software can replace faces in video with a young woman's, closing the last gap of "can't do video"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-38">[10-38]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

The case belongs in this book not because it is lurid but because it demonstrates a transferable structure: **AI turns one-to-one emotional labor into a persona pool that can be instantiated in bulk.** The cost of detection for risk and compliance rises accordingly — and the compliant version of the same technique in ordinary commercial settings, such as AI support or shopping assistants that do not disclose their machine identity, is precisely the adjacent risk regulators are watching.

**Franchise bubbles: the channel had not matured, but the channel resellers had.** While brands were still wondering whether GEO works at all, a cohort of vendors had already turned the business into recruiting city agents: heavy franchise advertising on Douyin and Xiaohongshu, pulling the existing client bases of tax advisory, advertising, and business-services firms into distribution. The pitch is usually "zero franchise fee," but initial inventory purchases, compute prepayments, system fees, and deposits ensure that something collects the money. The core problem is a supply-demand mismatch — GEO genuinely suits businesses with an established brand, users who really do ask AI questions, and the capacity to keep producing credible content over time, whereas the regional agents most easily reach restaurants, renovation firms, training schools, and small workshops; even if a vendor's ten designed questions show 100% mention rates, that does not mean any meaningful number of consumers actually asked them<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#3-77">[3-77]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

The shared lesson of the two examples: **when a capability combines a high-demand narrative with a low verification threshold, what matures first is usually not the service but the scams built around it.** The direct implication for customer operators is to ask first how a channel's effect is independently verified; a channel that cannot answer that question should not enter the budget, however complete its story.

## 10.12 The Liability of AI-Facing: "The Chatbot Did It" Is No Longer a Defense

The earlier part of Chapter 10 dealt with the consequences of AI saying the wrong thing. This round of legal developments nails the conclusion down: **a company is responsible for what its own AI says, even when the content is fabricated.**

Two rulings reach the same conclusion. First (Canada, 2024): a traveler asked an airline's website chatbot about bereavement fares, and the chatbot said they could be claimed retroactively when the actual policy required applying before travel; the airline refused a refund and argued that "the chatbot is a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions," which the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal (Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149) rejected outright, finding negligent misrepresentation. Second (Germany, 2026): a cosmetic-surgery clinic was sued after its AI chatbot described the practitioner with a nonexistent professional medical title, and the Oberlandesgericht Hamm, on May 12, 2026 (Az. 4 UKl 3/25), ruled that the chatbot is not an independent third party but a tool of the operator, whose statements are directly attributable to the company — even if the AI hallucinated or mishandled correct input — on the grounds that the operator defined the bot's scope, could reprogram it afterward, and had sufficient control over the system<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-39">[10-39]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The individual amounts are small, but the principle is large: an automated agent's statements are the company's statements.

The German ruling has a harder compliance backdrop. ePrivacy's practical analysis notes that the court treated the erroneous chatbot response as the company's own misleading commercial practice under Sections 5(1) and 5(2), No. 3 of the German Unfair Competition Act (UWG); the overlay of regulatory change is that Article 50(1) of the EU AI Act took effect on August 2, 2026, requiring transparent disclosure that a user "is interacting with AI"; and at the e-commerce and consumer-law level, false statements about goods, prices, or delivery can trigger not only cease-and-desist letters but contractual obligations, warranty claims, damages, or fines<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-40">[10-40]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. For merchants deploying AI support or sales agents, **"the AI got it wrong" is not just a brand risk but a direct compliance risk subject to enforcement and litigation.**

The official Chinese position has tightened in step. A China Consumers Association (CCA) advisory of September 2, reported by Science and Technology Daily via Xinhua, reminds consumers to treat general generative AI's "informational and referential" nature rationally: for important consumption information such as prices and fees, contract terms, after-sales service, and legal liability, decisions should not rely on AI-generated content alone. The CCA splits AI-related consumer problems into two categories: first, general generative AI should be clearly framed as referential to avoid misleading decisions — in practice there have been unsupported blanket promises, overconfident judgments about litigation outcomes, wrong jurisdiction guidance, and even fabricated nonexistent payments or case filings; second, a company deploying AI customer service cannot weaken its responsibility through technical intervention — there have been price statements inconsistent with reality, inaccurate interpretations of discount rules, insufficient notice of important restrictions, and some operators evading responsibility by citing "AI auto-generation" or "system auto-reply." It urges operators to establish a responsibility-acceptance mechanism for AI-support responses on price, fees, and after-sales matters<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-41">[10-41]</a></sup>[official claims].

The CCA's complaint-case compilation gives concrete detail. Case one (June 28, 2026): Mr. Zhou mis-bought a different-region version of an item on a game platform and, having not claimed or used it, applied for a refund, but "received only AI customer-service replies" throughout, was repeatedly transferred between channels such as mini-programs and service accounts, and never reached a human, with the issue unresolved. Case three (May 5, 2026): Mr. Gong, choosing a car in a used-car app, was told by the platform's official AI support "Xiaogua" before paying a 2,000-yuan deposit that the 28,700-yuan price already included transfer and registration fees, and paid on that "official promise"; during the transaction the salesperson demanded an additional 1,000 yuan for transfer fees, leaving the consumer feeling that the platform's AI support and its staff gave contradictory information and that he had decided on the basis of wrong information<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-42">[10-42]</a></sup>[official claims]. **"Receiving only AI replies and being unable to reach a human" is itself a service defect, and an AI-support response is binding.**

The harm on the user side is equally concrete. A report by Legal Daily via Xinhua gives a case: on May 18, 2026, a consumer in Zhenjiang, Jiangsu, used an AI app to book a restaurant and received the affirmative reply "reservation successful, enjoy your meal," with details of arrival time and party size and the note that "showing this AI app's reservation at the door is enough to be seated"; at the restaurant the consumer was told the venue did not support booking through that app and no reservation had actually been made, and the app's support admitted the platform could not pass information to third parties and had no real booking function. Experts advise users to "check the label, protect data, verify sources, and follow the law," and platforms to flag the possibility of "AI hallucination" in user agreements, label AI-generated content, and design easy complaint and correction mechanisms<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-43">[10-43]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

## 10.13 The Shutdown of Anthropomorphic Agents and the Mandate to Label

**Chinese platforms have drawn a line for "anthropomorphic agents."** Sina Finance, citing Southern Metropolis Daily, reports that three leading Chinese AI apps collectively took their "agent" features offline just before a new regulatory rule took effect: Doubao issued a "Doubao Agent Feature Shutdown Notice," saying that due to product adjustments the agent feature would go offline on July 15, 2026; Qwen sent a matching notice that after July 15 users could no longer access agent configurations or conversation history; and Tencent's Yuanbao had already taken the feature offline on June 30. The shutdown dates coincide with the effective date (July 15, 2026) of the Interim Measures for the Administration of Anthropomorphic Interactive AI Services — jointly issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China and four other departments on April 10, applicable to services provided to the domestic public that "simulate natural-person personality traits, thinking patterns, and communication styles in sustained emotional interaction"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-44">[10-44]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. For merchants doing "agent-based acquisition," this means the compliance boundary for anthropomorphic agents on Chinese platforms has been redrawn, and third-party agents may be required to migrate into more regulated apps.

**The labeling mandate has already landed at the platform level as graded penalties.** Drawing on the Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content (effective September 1, 2025) and the three platforms' community rules, ByeRisk compares Douyin, Xiaohongshu, and WeChat Channels: for explicit labels, Douyin uses an "AI-generated" corner tag at the lower left plus a watermark in the first three seconds, Xiaohongshu a horizontal banner atop the note, and WeChat Channels a corner tag at the upper right plus a pinned comment; implicit labeling requires reading EXIF/C2PA and other metadata and running content-fingerprint or similarity detection. Penalties are tiered: L1 (missing label: auto-labeling plus a private message), L2 (repeated misses: seven to fourteen days of downranking plus frozen traffic packages), L3 (deliberate concealment: removal plus a ban of at least seven days plus loss of commercial rights), and L4 (illegality: permanent ban plus referral to police). The article records that on August 7, 2026, Xiaohongshu upgraded its AI Governance Rules Announcement, becoming the first of the three platforms to extend the labeling obligation from individual posts to the account level (accounts operating AI virtual-human personas must enable the "AI virtual human" profession label on their profile)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#10-45">[10-45]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **When "disclosing AI" is upgraded from advice to an admission and distribution condition, the compliance cost of acquisition content is formally entered into the media model** — the "channel falsification" of Section 3.14 also holds on the compliance dimension.

## 10.14 Summary

The boundaries of AI-powered customer lifecycle management condense into five points:

1. **Backlash**: AI outbound is killing cold email — channel dividends are one-time, and restraint is a strategy;
2. **Hallucination**: AI's mistakes are the company's mistakes — Air Canada paid, and the Hangzhou case was exempted (with disclosure); guardrails determine liability;
3. **Compliance**: regulators have caught up with AI selling — FTC/FCC/SEC/EU AI Act/China's labeling measures, and AI washing is illegal;
4. **The human-machine boundary**: AI impersonating real people is a global red line — trust is the foundation of customer operations;
5. **Liability**: companies bear responsibility, but AI liability insurance has appeared — a risk being insurable shows the industry is maturing.

The final chapter looks to the future: when customers themselves become AI, what will customer operations become?
