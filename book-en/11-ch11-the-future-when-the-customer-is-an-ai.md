# Chapter 11 The Future: When the Customer Is an AI

In February 2026, CMSWire published a long essay titled "The Machine Customer Economy Has Begun." It offered a prediction that makes many operators uncomfortable: your next batch of customers may not be human.

"Machine customers" are smart devices and AI assistants that carry out service activities on behalf of humans — filing repair orders, renewing supplies, and comparison shopping. Gartner's prediction is more specific: by 2026, 20% of inbound customer-service contacts will come from machine customers<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-22">[11-22]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

This chapter is about the future. But one clarification first: the future is not distant — it is already happening.

## 11.1 Agentic Commerce: AI Agents Shop for People

The definition of agentic commerce (agent-driven e-commerce) is simple: AI agents complete shopping on behalf of consumers — the AI anticipates needs, searches across and compares prices, negotiates, and places orders on their behalf<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-06">[11-06]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Scale predictions (cross-validated across institutions):
- McKinsey: agentic commerce could generate $1 trillion in U.S. retail revenue by 2030<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-06">[11-06]</a></sup> [third-party verified];
- Morgan Stanley: by 2030, nearly half of U.S. consumers will use AI agents, adding up to $115 billion in new spending to U.S. e-commerce<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#4-11">[4-11]</a></sup> [third-party verified];
- Gartner: by 2028, AI agents will intermediate more than $15 trillion in B2B spending — 90% of B2B purchases will be handled by agents<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-13">[11-13]</a></sup> [third-party verified];
- Deloitte: transaction traffic from AI assistants to U.S. retail sites is surging, and agentic commerce is redefining retail economics<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-05">[11-05]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Signs of real-world adoption have already appeared: in October 2025, Walmart was the first to partner with OpenAI, allowing users to complete purchases directly inside ChatGPT through Instant Checkout; a month later, Target followed with its own AI shopping experience<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-16">[11-16]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Salesforce's 2025 holiday-season data shows: AI/agents accounted for a 20% share of retail, driving $262 billion; brands that deployed agents grew 59%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-17">[11-17]</a></sup> [official claims].

The shift in shopping patterns deserves one more emphasis: customers no longer "browse"; they "brief" — issuing a requirement instruction such as "I want a waterproof jacket for under €200," and the AI agent interprets, compares, and recommends across retailers<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-25">[11-25]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The web page is no longer the interface; an AI decision layer has become the new intermediary between customers and every retailer.

## 11.2 When the Buyer Is an AI, What Do You Sell?

This is a double shock for operators. The good news: AI agents are tireless 24/7 buyers that close deals efficiently. The bad news: **AI agents recognize only data, not brands** — when the buyer is an AI, human emotional assets like "brand affinity" are of no use.

So why would an AI agent choose you? ML6's answer, in four characters' worth of meaning: **machine-readable**. Product data (how structured it is, inventory truthfulness, price transparency, GTIN identifiers), reliable commerce signals (real-time inventory/pricing), and interoperable protocols and identity layers — "product data is upgraded from a hygiene task to a strategic asset"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-25">[11-25]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. BCG's reminder is just as critical: a retailer's most valuable customers may no longer be human — AI agents (embedded in Perplexity, ChatGPT, and Google Gemini) are taking over the discover, compare, decide, and order stages<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-14">[11-14]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

An action checklist for operators:
1. **Data is the new storefront** — your product pages are for people to look at, but what AI agents read is your structured data feed;
2. **Trust signals must be machine-verifiable** — humans look at "brand reputation"; AI looks at "verifiable review data, warranty terms, and return policies";
3. **Prepare for agent-to-agent transactions** — frameworks for machine-to-machine negotiation and data verification in B2B are taking shape<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-13">[11-13]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

## 11.3 GEO: Being Cited by AI Is the New Channel

When customers — human or AI — get answers through ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Gemini, whether your brand gets cited becomes a matter of life and death.

As early as 2024, Gartner predicted that traditional search-engine traffic would fall 25% by 2026<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-12">[11-12]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The actual citation data cuts even deeper: original research by Boring Marketing (built on 14,750 real buyer questions tested) found that 53% of brands are completely invisible across the four mainstream AI platforms (ChatGPT/Perplexity/Claude/Gemini), with an overall citation rate of just 14%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-03">[11-03]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Being cited by AI is not mysticism; it is observable and optimizable (Chapter 3 covered the case of Glasp doing AEO by reading its own server logs). Thirty-five percent of U.S. consumers already use AI at the product-discovery stage<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-23">[11-23]</a></sup> [third-party verified] — GEO (generative engine optimization) is becoming a channel as important as SEO.

## 11.4 Trust: The Stronger the AI, the More Valuable the Human Touch

A chapter about the future should not be only about technology; it must also be about people — because the stronger the technology, the higher the premium on the "human touch."

The data paints two pictures that seem contradictory but are actually consistent:

**Consumers trust AI to help them buy better.** BCG's global survey (9,000+ respondents across 9 countries): more than 60% of consumers highly trust GenAI's results<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-08">[11-08]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Edelman's Trust Barometer shows brand trust surging in recent years — 80% of people trust the brands they use<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-01">[11-01]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**But in customer service, consumers would rather talk to a real person.** Metrigy's research: 85% of consumers prefer interacting with a human rather than an AI agent in customer service<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-15">[11-15]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. A Medallia report predicts that nearly half of consumers believe that ten years from now they will no longer deal with any company directly — AI agents will do it for them — and that "human service" itself will become premium-priced: more than two-thirds of consumers believe human service will become an optional premium offering<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-24">[11-24]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Taken together, the two pictures point to a clear future: **routine transactions go to AI (consumers welcome this); at critical moments, they want a human (consumers vote with their feet)**. Human service will not disappear; it will become a "luxury good" — and once AI handles 95% of standardized interactions, the remaining 5% of human interactions are the brand's entire battleground for differentiation. The boundary described in Chapter 2 will be drawn even wider in the future: AI handles scale, humans handle trust, and the premium on trust keeps rising.

## 11.5 The Machine-Customer Ledger: Early Numbers

The previous sections covered trends. This section pulls "machine customers" from narrative back to numbers.

One vendor compilation gathers the estimates of various institutions on agentic commerce: Bain forecasts the U.S. market at $300–500 billion by 2030, 15%–25% of e-commerce; Gartner predicts 20% of digital commerce transactions will go through AI platforms or agents by 2030; Morgan Stanley forecasts 10%–20% of U.S. e-commerce sales driven by agents; McKinsey estimates it can create about $1 trillion in orchestrated U.S. retail revenue and $3–5 trillion globally; J.P. Morgan expects up to 25% of U.S. online sales to flow via agents by 2030, concentrated in recurring, low-risk categories such as groceries and subscriptions. The consumer side is moving too: by 2030 nearly 50% of online shoppers are expected to use AI agents and contribute about 25% of spending, adding roughly $115 billion to U.S. e-commerce; Kearney estimates 60% of consumers expect to use AI agents in the next 12 months and 73% are familiar with AI tools; 63% of European consumers already use AI for shopping<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-26">[11-26]</a></sup> [official claims]. These numbers come from different institutions with different methodologies, but the direction is the same — **"agents as the shopping entry point" is no longer a forecast but a ledger being cashed out.**

Merchant adoption is running ahead too: a payment provider's report says 42% of U.K. and U.S. merchants have already moved first on "AI agent commerce," with AI entry points reshaping the discover-compare-order chain<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-27">[11-27]</a></sup> [official claims].

For brands, the real strategic question is not "whether to enter this entry point" but "once I'm in, am I still the protagonist." IBM's Institute for Business Value sums it up in one word — **own**: brands should "own" the agentic commerce experience rather than being intermediated by AI entry points. When purchase decisions are made by consumers' AI agents, brands' control over discovery, comparison, and purchase weakens, and they must win back the "right to be recommended" through data and experience<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-28">[11-28]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. This echoes the conclusion in 11.2: when the buyer is an AI, what you sell is a set of data and experiences that machines can read, verify, and trust.

Taken together with the earlier sections, the machine-customer ledger carries two numbers: an incremental market measured in hundreds of billions of dollars, and an existing risk of being "intermediated." The operator's job is to keep ownership of the customer relationship between those two numbers.

## 11.6 Protocols and Admission Tickets: The Barrier to Entry in the Machine-Customer Era

Section 11.1 described the shape of agentic commerce. By 2026 it has shifted from "who can build it" to "who can be plugged in" — protocols, policies, and fee schedules now form the admission ticket to the machine-customer era.

**Is the protocol war over?** In April 2026, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Salesforce, and Stripe joined the Universal Commerce Protocol technical committee, forming a ten-seat governance body with Google, Shopify, Etsy, Target, and Wayfair. UCP covers the entire shopping chain from discovery and purchase to after-sales support and is endorsed by more than 20 ecosystem players. But the author's judgment is that "the protocol war is over; the commercial war is not": UCP's advantage is not that its checkout API is pleasant, but that it can accommodate the complexity of real commerce — an agent must be able to ask on the user's behalf whether these three items can be bought with this coupon, shipped to this address, paid with this wallet, and stacked with loyalty benefits, while respecting the merchant's tax, risk, fulfillment, and return rules. The counter-evidence is equally clear: Amazon has not opened its marketplace to external agents, Meta has not handed Instagram commerce to a neutral protocol, and OpenAI shifted ChatGPT shopping in March 2026 from its first version of instant checkout to "product discovery plus merchant-controlled checkout"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-29">[11-29]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Protocols imply a budget reshuffle.** A comparative guide notes that ACP (OpenAI with Stripe, launched September 2025, living inside ChatGPT) and UCP (Google-led, covering AI Mode and Gemini) serve different purposes rather than competing — ChatGPT excels at conversational product discovery while Google AI Mode captures high-intent search demand, so most brands will need to support both. The real implementation judgment is that agentic commerce is **infrastructure-first, not content-first**: AI shopping agents do not read editorial content, they read structured data, evaluate feeds, and call APIs, so budget should shift from top-of-funnel content to bottom-of-funnel infrastructure — product data completeness, schema markup, feed hygiene, checkout API reliability, and server-side tracking. The same piece concedes that early agentic commerce "sells but cannot be measured": existing analytics stacks were designed for clicks and sessions and cannot fully attribute orders brought by AI agents<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-30">[11-30]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Platforms are already writing rules for agents.** Amazon updated its Business Solutions Agreement effective March 4, 2026, adding a dedicated agent policy for the first time and imposing new requirements on AI use and automated systems<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-31">[11-31]</a></sup> [official claims]. The detailed requirements include: defining an "AI seller agent" as a system that makes or executes decisions on a seller account without real-time human input for each action; requiring all automation to run through registered SP-API applications, with browser automation and Seller Central scraping explicitly prohibited; mandating a 12-month audit trail for automated actions and self-identification as an automated system; and requiring human authorization for high-impact actions (for example, price changes above 20% within 24 hours)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-32">[11-32]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. The strategic implication matters more than the technical detail: generative search and shopping agents often cannot see a marketplace's on-site listings, so brands must build complete product content on their own channels and treat marketplaces as conversion venues rather than discovery venues — otherwise they go silent at the AI discovery stage.

**In the Chinese market the money is re-routing too.** Effective August 10, 2026, Douyin's local-services arm applies a separate fee schedule to hotel orders completed through the Doubao entry point: an 11.4% software service fee plus a 0.6% payment processing fee, about 12% combined. Doubao's response is that its local-services business currently runs no paid promotion and merchants cannot influence recommendations or ranking by paying; only a channel service fee is charged after an order is completed<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-33">[11-33]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Simultaneous data shows the scale of the entry point: as of June 2026, AI-native apps reached 499 million monthly active users, up 85.4% year over year, with Doubao first at 382 million. The industry reading is that the AI assistant's role in local-services transaction chains has formally shifted from a platform's "organic traffic entry point" to an **independent transaction channel** — though binding AI recommendations to commercial interests faces a long-term "trust gap."

**Payment infrastructure is moving faster than protocols.** Alipay reported that AI agent payments have passed 300 million transactions and support 95% of general-purpose agent frameworks, and launched Token Pay and an AI wallet to complete a full-stack AI-native payment product matrix alongside its earlier "AI Pay" and "AI Collect"; on the protocol side it upgraded China's open trust framework for agentic commerce and was first to build A2A (agent-to-agent) and A2M (agent-to-machine) payment capability, while on the merchant side it released its first merchant operations agent<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-34">[11-34]</a></sup> [official claims]. The cross-device layer has been filled in as well: Alipay released China's first multi-agent cross-device interconnection protocol system covering three layers — how to talk, how to connect, and how to act — prompted precisely by the first generation of "Doubao phones" being collectively blocked by major apps for using simulated taps, cross-app jumps, and screen reading to bypass restrictions. In one partnership between Alipay and a phone maker, the latter's AI assistant connected nearly 200 Alipay services, adding more than 2 million to Alipay's activity on that brand's handsets<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-35">[11-35]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. On the cross-border side, Ant International's Agentic Mobile Protocol onboarded 10 digital wallets and 7 acquiring institutions covering 1.5 billion users at launch and coordinated with two major card networks on a Know-Your-Agent (KYA) trust framework, aiming to simplify agent onboarding and identification across networks while each party retains its own security verification and decision processes<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-36">[11-36]</a></sup> [official claims].

Taken together, 2026 offers three admission tickets — which protocol you connect to, which agent policy you comply with, and which fee and identity framework you accept. None of them belongs to the marketing department, yet each directly determines whether a brand is visible to machine customers.

**The card networks pushed "machine payments" from consumers to enterprises.** In June 2026 Mastercard launched Agent Pay for Machines, positioned to let enterprises support secure, continuous machine payments across cards, accounts, and currencies, with the served parties extending from AI agents to devices and software programs — the second step, after the consumer-facing Agent Pay of April 2025, in which the payment network pushes agent payments toward enterprise-side machine transactions<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-41">[11-41]</a></sup>[official claims]. Visa started on the same day with a different direction: Intelligent Commerce positions itself as the trust layer for agentic shopping, sharing verifiable credentials among issuers, merchants, and AI platforms about whether an agent is user-authorized, what it may buy, and up to what limit; in April 2026 it added a protocol-agnostic merchant connection product designed to accept agent orders from multiple protocols (Trusted Agent Protocol and others) at once<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-42">[11-42]</a></sup>[official claims]. Read together, the card networks are not competing over whose agent is better but over **whose authorization and verification standard gets adopted by everyone else** — a standards contest is a channel contest. For merchants, the real preparatory action is connecting a verifiable "agent identity and authorization" chain rather than adapting platform by platform.

## 11.7 Machine Customers' Money and Trust

**The money is real.** Amazon's CEO told an earnings call that its AI shopping assistant is on pace to drive more than $10 billion in incremental annual sales, and that customers who use it are 60% more likely to complete a purchase than those who do not. The measurement basis is worth noting: the $10 billion is measured by Amazon's internal "downstream impact" metric using a seven-day rolling attribution model to capture delayed conversions — the assistant's value is counted as the indirect sales it triggers, not direct clicks<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-37">[11-37]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. That also implies something: once the assistant itself becomes an acquisition and advertising vehicle, the measurement basis has to be redesigned, or this value stays invisible in the reporting.

**Both volume and quality of the channel are changing.** Adobe Analytics data shows AI referral traffic to U.S. retail sites in July 2026 was up 62% year over year and up 1,219% cumulatively compared with October 2024, when generative platforms began to spread; more important is quality — in July, AI-referred visitors converted 60% better than non-AI traffic (54% in May)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-38">[11-38]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. A retailer-side data compilation offers another basis: AI referral traffic to U.S. retail sites grew 393% year over year in the first quarter of 2026, with AI-referred visitors spending 48% more time and viewing 13% more pages; on the consumer side, 42% used an AI shopping tool in the past month, 5% have let a fully autonomous AI agent place an order, and 85% say AI improved their shopping experience — while 42% of shopping-related AI conversations are "selection and inspiration" queries rather than direct orders<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-39">[11-39]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. High intent matters more than high volume — but that requires brands to first fix landing-page validation, product merchandising logic, and cross-channel attribution.

**Trust is the only speed limit.** A brand-side guide cites survey figures that must be read together: consumer adoption of agentic shopping is projected to rise from 19% to 46% by the end of 2026, but only 10% of consumers are willing to let an agent operate fully independently — the vast majority still want human confirmation in the purchase decision — while 83% of consumers have concerns about privacy, data misuse, and unauthorized marketing<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-40">[11-40]</a></sup> [official claims]. This matches the judgment in Section 11.4: the expansion rate of machine customers is set not by model capability but by how clearly authorization and refunds are defined. Brands face two risk surfaces — visibility risk (if agents do not recommend you, you do not make the shortlist) and trust risk (unclear control mechanisms directly suppress adoption).

**Demand-side conservatism is moving far more slowly than the infrastructure.** Put several independent data sets side by side and the gap is plain. Gartner's consumer research concludes that only 11% of U.S. consumers are willing to let AI make purchase decisions, and advises marketers to design AI shopping tools primarily as research support rather than as the decision-maker<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-43">[11-43]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. Payment company Checkout.com's research fills in the middle ground: 33% of consumers expect at least 10% of their purchases to be AI-driven within a year, and 72% of merchants believe consumers will adopt agent-led shopping faster than most merchants are prepared for; on the trust side, three figures form the drag — 27% of consumers trust no institution to run an AI shopping agent, 24% say they will never delegate a purchase to AI, and 57% are willing to let an AI agent switch brands for a better price<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-44">[11-44]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The payment networks' own forecasts are the most expansive: a Mastercard report predicts that by 2030 more than one in ten online shoppers will routinely use an AI agent to choose and pay for them<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-45">[11-45]</a></sup>[official claims] — a claim whose provenance should be labeled clearly, since it is a payment network's self-interested forecast: the pipes only carry volume once they are built.

**Read together, the four data sets point to one conclusion: infrastructure and expectations are running ahead of consumer authorization.** That gap in between is precisely what brands and platforms must fill together (authorization, revocability, auditability).

**On the B2B side, the migration path looks more like agent-to-agent.** Deloitte judges that the future form of B2B commerce is multi-agent systems researching, negotiating, purchasing, and selling on behalf of enterprises: buyers spend nearly 30% more budget with suppliers that offer an excellent buying experience, and the perception gap between buyers and sellers about the sales process is widening — a gap that, compounded by the maturation of agentic AI, creates a window for sellers to get ahead; the article also projects that if agents are orchestrated strategically and risks appropriately mitigated, the market value of AI agents could rise to $45 billion<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-46">[11-46]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **Sellers must be legible to two kinds of readers at once** — with a website and sales process a human can understand, and with specifications, prices, terms, and inventory data an agent can read and compare directly.

**A magnitude that agencies can plan around has appeared.** Several agencies estimate that Perplexity's shopping business has reached an annualized GMV run rate approaching $2 billion; Perplexity has not confirmed the figure, so it should be read as a directional signal rather than an audited result. The growth comes from a change in answer format: when users ask shopping questions, the answer carries product cards alongside the usual sourced response, and Perplexity says these cards are relevance-ranked non-sponsored recommendations rather than paid slots. Transactions are carried by the subscription tier, and a free merchant program feeds retailers' (including Shopify stores') real-time product data into its index<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-47">[11-47]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The significance lies in entry rules that invert search advertising: **free onboarding, ranked by relevance rather than bid** — which means a brand's "spend" here looks more like a contest of product data and content than a contest of budget.

## 11.8 The Layering of the Protocol Stack and the 2026 Retreat

Section 11.6 was about admission tickets. This round's details are more interesting: **the protocols are being layered, while 2025's product claims are already out of date.**

**Three standards are not on the same layer; don't treat them as a three-way choice.** A merchant guide lays out the 2026 agentic-commerce standards clearly: UCP (Google with Shopify, Etsy, Walmart, Target, and others) covers the full journey from discovery and cart to checkout and post-purchase, with merchants publishing capabilities through a `/.well-known/ucp` JSON manifest and supporting REST, A2A, and MCP transports; ACP (co-built by OpenAI and Stripe, open-sourced September 29, 2025) handles checkout execution, its first landing being Instant Checkout in ChatGPT, with Etsy the first merchant and more than a million Shopify merchants later connected; and AP2, now governed by the FIDO Alliance, handles payment authorization. The article notes that ACP "outlived its own product" — OpenAI scaled back Instant Checkout in March 2026, and the protocol retreated from a checkout product to a "pipe"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-48">[11-48]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

A cross-border payments firm's explainer pinpoints ACP's key change: in March 2026 OpenAI scaled back native instant checkout and routed purchases back into merchant apps inside ChatGPT, "to some degree returning checkout control to merchants"; UCP, by contrast, takes an "open orchestration" route, with product retrieval queried by different agents across multiple platforms, authorization information and intent data transmitted with the request for the merchant's own risk checks, and the transaction still completed through the merchant's existing checkout page or payment service provider — the protocol serving as "a universal interaction language rather than the final landing vehicle"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-49">[11-49]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. An architectural view offers a cleaner contrast: UCP is decentralized (merchants publish capabilities any agent can discover and transact with), ACP is platform-mediated (merchants submit data to OpenAI, which ChatGPT presents), and in practice merchants will likely implement both to maximize distribution, "just as retailers list on both Google Shopping and Amazon"; MCP is not a commerce protocol but a general tool-integration layer, relevant to commerce because both UCP and ACP explicitly support MCP as a transport binding<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-50">[11-50]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**The product-level correction matters more: don't treat 2025's announcements as the status quo.** An explainer around OpenAI's March 2026 update says plainly that the first version of Instant Checkout did not offer the flexibility it wanted, so the direction shifted to product discovery and letting merchants use their own checkout experience, with Instant Checkout possibly available only for "eligible products and merchants"; any unverifiable order, payment, or delivery question should go to the responsible team<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-51">[11-51]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. A product-feed vendor's roundup adds the operational detail: Google's legacy Content API for Shopping is set to shut down on August 18, 2026, so programmatic access must migrate to the Merchant API; in May 2026 Google added six optional "conversational attributes" (question_and_answer, related_product, variant_option, item_group_title, popularity_rank, document_link), not required but giving AI shopping more structured context<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-52">[11-52]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **The same Merchant Center feed is also read by Microsoft Copilot — the structure of the feed determines whether AI can understand you.**

The platforms' official positions give the channel differences: Shopify's blog explains that Perplexity shopping is driven by a free merchant program using a Google Shopping-format feed; Perplexity completes in-conversation purchases via PayPal; Google AI Mode supports Shopify-powered in-conversation checkout, with eligible, opted-in orders flowing into the Shopify back end like ordinary orders; and ChatGPT presents products through Shopify Catalog and directs buyers via an in-app browser to the merchant's own store. Shopify merchants can be discovered in ChatGPT automatically through Shopify Catalog, with no extra setup<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-53">[11-53]</a></sup>[official claims].

## 11.9 How Much Room Consumers Give AI Shopping Agents to Make a Mistake

Section 11.7 said trust is the only rate limiter. Consumer surveys in the first half of 2026 measured that limiter's value: **the tolerance is extremely low.**

A survey commissioned by payments-technology firm ACI Worldwide and run by YouGov among 2,080 UK adults (June 19–22, 2026) delivers the headline: **six in ten UK consumers say that if an AI shopping agent makes one mistake, they will stop using it**; consumers have clear concerns about "control, money, and who is responsible" — that is, about an agent spending on their behalf and who answers when it errs<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-54">[11-54]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. For merchants preparing to hand the closing step to an agent, this is a "near-zero error tolerance" demand-side signal.

Klaviyo's reading of its 2026 AI Consumer Trends Report gives tiered trust: only 13% of consumers "fully trust" AI, 36% "somewhat trust" it, and 30% are neutral; on shopping recommendations specifically, 85% have at least some trust that AI provides accurate, personalized recommendations, and 39% have bought an AI-recommended product in the past six months; but only 54% trust AI to provide good customer service through a conversational agent — **trust in the "recommendation" scenario is clearly higher than in the "service" scenario.** On the brand side, 32% of consumers would trust a brand less for using AI-generated content, and nearly one in five sees low-quality or formulaic AI content from brands weekly. Klaviyo's advice is not to remove human review and to route complex support requests back to people<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-55">[11-55]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

Alchemer's 2026 Retail Report (1,002 U.S. shoppers) is titled "retail AI adoption outpaces consumer trust": 48.5% of shoppers have used an AI tool to research a purchase in the past year; by age, 74% of 18–29-year-olds have done so versus only 20% of those 61 and older, and the youngest group is nine times as likely as the oldest to "discover their most recent purchase through an AI tool"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-56">[11-56]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **Read alongside ACI's six-in-ten error tolerance, this gives the real constraint on the expansion of machine customers: it is not that the model is too weak, but that users give the agent a trial-and-error budget of exactly one.**

**And autonomy is already tiered inside products.** A postmortem on Amazon's shopping agent Rufus lays out three product lines: Q&A, Buy for Me (letting Rufus browse and compare off-Amazon items, with the final purchase still requiring user approval), and Auto Buy (fully autonomous, limited to Prime members and FBA items, with a default six-month monitoring window that buys automatically when the price is hit — Amazon says users save about 20% on average). For sellers, Rufus favors items with strong reviews and clear descriptions, sparse or low-quality listings become "invisible to Rufus," and the AI Q&A section has shifted from an afterthought to a primary data source for AI recommendations<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-57">[11-57]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. This closes the loop with Section 11.6: **before machine customers, visibility comes from the completeness of data and reviews, not from the media budget.**

## 11.10 The Second Inventory of the Protocol Stack: The ACP–UCP Split

Sections 11.6 and 11.8 covered protocols and the pullback. This round's increment is **an engineering-level comparison of the two routes**, which turns "which protocol do we pick" from a matter of judgment into a matter of scheduling.

**The structural difference between the two routes.** An engineering practice piece compares OpenAI's ACP with Google's UCP layer by layer. **ACP (Agentic Commerce Protocol)** was released by OpenAI and Stripe in September 2025 under Apache 2.0 and consists of three parts: a product feed (merchants push structured catalogue data, where OpenAI is investing the most), checkout sessions (REST contracts to create, update, and complete a checkout, which merchants may accept or decline while retaining merchant-of-record status), and delegated payment; the latest stable spec is dated April 17, 2026, with the United States first, and because in-app checkout struggled, most buyers have been routed back to merchants' own checkout since March 2026. **UCP (Universal Commerce Protocol)** was launched by Google on January 11, 2026 at NRF, co-developed with Shopify, Walmart, Target, and others, covering discovery, comparison, cart, checkout, order tracking, and post-purchase across the full lifecycle; its architecture is decentralized — merchants publish a JSON profile at `/.well-known/ucp` and agents call merchant endpoints based on it; it sits alongside MCP, A2A, and AP2 rather than replacing them, its latest spec is dated August 25, 2026, it is live in the United States with Canada and Australia in progress, and its account system uses OAuth 2.0 for identity linking. **The split in one sentence: ACP is a centralized pipe that feeds the catalogue into ChatGPT; UCP is a capability contract the agent discovers on your own domain** — the former's data freshness depends on syncing on a platform outside your control, the latter's on your own APIs; ACP keeps conversion on your site, UCP can complete the transaction inside Google<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-58">[11-58]</a></sup>[third-party verified].

**A side branch: four-way competition in payment protocols.** Another comparison sets MPP, ACP, AP2, and x402 side by side with their respective positioning and technical form: ACP defines the checkout interaction between agent and merchant through four RESTful endpoints (create, update, complete, cancel checkout), with payment handled via SharedPaymentTokens<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-59">[11-59]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The timeline has also been pieced together: in September 2025 Google launched AP2 with more than 60 partners including American Express and Mastercard, standardizing through an open protocol how AI agents complete financial transactions securely on a user's behalf; less than two weeks later OpenAI launched Instant Checkout (with Stripe and Shopify, generally available for purchases from Etsy sellers)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-60">[11-60]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **The closeness of those two dates is itself the clearest footnote to this competition: standards are grabbed and published, not slowly negotiated.**

**On the payment side, the action centres on tokens.** Stripe announced an expansion of its Shared Payment Tokens — SPTs let an AI agent initiate payment with the customer's authorization and preferred method without exposing underlying credentials, and Etsy and URBN (including Anthropologie, Free People, and Urban Outfitters) have adopted them broadly; the new capability connects SPTs to network-led agent payment capabilities, namely Mastercard Agent Pay and Visa Intelligent Commerce, as well as buy-now-pay-later<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-61">[11-61]</a></sup>[official]. The payment provider Checkout.com proposes five building blocks for security, and the first deserves particular note: **agent identity — a "know your agent" (KYA) framework modelled on KYC, giving each AI agent a distinct digital identity so merchants, issuers, and card networks can recognize agent-initiated transactions**; the others are dedicated credentials, secure authentication, fraud prevention, and end-to-end observability<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-62">[11-62]</a></sup>[official claims].

**The Chinese-language framing of this is the most thorough.** A People's Daily Online report defines agent-initiated payment as a change in payment logic rather than "AI-assisted ordering": with the user pre-setting permissions, budget, scenario, and rules, the AI agent independently completes requirement parsing, price comparison and ordering, fund transfer, and reconciliation, with no need for the consumer to confirm each step. Tian Lihui (Chinese: 田利辉), dean of the Institute of Finance and Development at Nankai University (Chinese: 南开大学), offers three formulations worth reading word for word: payment moves from "the human confirms" to "the system judges intent," from "human-led" to "human–machine co-decision"; **the transaction unit upgrades from a product to a task — the user no longer buys a single item but a complete solution, with "travel" replacing "airfare"**; and payment logic evolves from bilateral transactions into multilateral network coordination<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-63">[11-63]</a></sup>[state media].

**The operational implication lives in the rollout path.** The engineering comparison offers a 12-week sequence: weeks 1–4 fix the data layer (variant modelling, GTINs, inventory sync latency, rewriting copy in conversational attributes, exposing real-time price and inventory APIs); weeks 4–8 pick the first protocol by traffic source (Google search and Shopping ads to UCP; research-mode shopping in ChatGPT to an ACP feed; on Shopify use Agentic Storefronts directly; self-built or headless, start with UCP and then ACP); weeks 8–12 abstract catalogue, cart, and checkout into an internal "commerce capability service," expose it first as MCP tools, and add thin ACP/UCP adapters on top — **the goal being to avoid rewriting checkout with every spec revision**<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-58">[11-58]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. That advice matches the foundation-layer logic of Chapter 7: **protocols change; the capability layer does not have to.**

## 11.11 Converging Distribution Entries, and the New Wording of the Ticket In

The last piece of the machine-customer chapter is **where these agents get found**.

**Stores are converging from fragmentation into a few entry points.** One study traces the 2026 convergence of AI agent marketplaces and directories, naming Microsoft Marketplace, Salesforce Agentforce, Google Gemini Enterprise, ChatGPT Workspace Agents, and the Claude Skills directory, and putting the open Agent Skills ecosystem at more than 1.2 million entries<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-64">[11-64]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. On the channel side, the move of the period is consolidation: Salesforce merged AgentExchange, Slack Marketplace, and the Agentforce ecosystem into a single entry point where customers can discover, buy, activate, and manage agents, apps, and capabilities<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-65">[11-65]</a></sup>[official claims]. **The cost and the benefit of convergence are the same fact: a listing's marginal exposure rises, and so does concentration of exposure to platform rule changes.** Compare the "directory dividend versus directory noise" data from Section 3.14 (the GPT Store hosted more than 3 million custom GPTs by early 2026, diluting the organic discoverability of any single entry) and a pragmatic conclusion follows: **listing is necessary but not sufficient; off-platform traffic and distribution beyond the charts are the real acquisition.** A directory does not make you visible; it only makes you eligible to be seen.

**Platforms have already written down how the ticket in works.** Amazon added specific clauses to its updated Business Solutions Agreement and Seller Central terms governing "automated systems and AI Agents acting on behalf of sellers," drawing the line cleanly: **scraping Seller Central for data is prohibited, while automation through the official SP-API is allowed**<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-66">[11-66]</a></sup>[third-party interpretation]. The implication extends beyond Amazon — **in the machine-customer era, compliance is not about whether you may automate but along which channel you automate**: automation through official interfaces is permitted, while simulated logins and scraping are not. Tool selection therefore directly determines account risk.

**And the machine customer has already arrived.** The shopping agents reviewed in Section 11.9 are already running three product lines at different levels of autonomy, and their user scale and the sales they drive are turning "being recommended by an agent" into a new ranking slot. Put that together with the consumer-trust data in Section 11.7 and the 2026 picture is complete: **agents buy, humans watch; platforms converge entry points and rewrite the rules; and what brands have to do is half making themselves callable in protocols and directories, and half showing real evidence to the people who come back to their own turf.** The first mirror in Chapter 3 and the last shelf in this chapter are, in fact, two ends of the same chain.

## 11.12 After the Cart Is Merged: A Payment Rail That Arrived First, Protocols That Arrived First

Section 11.3 covered "being cited by AI is a new channel," and 11.8 covered the protocol stack's layers and the 2026 retrenchment. This section looks at two moves in the autumn of 2026 — one from the shelf on the search side, one from the rail on the payment side.

**The shelf has been merged into a cross-retailer cart.** In October 2026 Google opened Universal Cart to U.S. users (previewed at I/O in May), available in Google Search's AI Mode and the Gemini app: a single cart can hold products from different retailers, track price and stock changes, suggest savings, and return to the merchant's own site to complete a purchase when needed. Retailers integrated or supported include Ulta Beauty, Walmart, Wayfair and some Shopify merchants, with Newegg the latest name to join UCP checkout in October. Google senior director of product management Suresh Ganapathy's example is concrete: save a Steve Madden bag in AI Mode, a Quince sweater and an Ulta lipstick in Gemini, and they are all still in the cart when you come back<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-67">[11-67]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **The implication is direct: once the shelf is merged into a cross-retailer cart, brands stop competing on "store-page conversion rate" and start competing on being included by the cart's recommendation logic.**

**China took another road: payments first.** A Chinese-language Business Wire release citing Ant Group data reports that in the week of February 5–11, 2026, Alipay AI Pay processed more than 120 million transactions<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-68">[11-68]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. The number matters less for its size than for its nature: **"AI places the order for the user" in China is no longer a demo but a payment rail carrying real volume, and it sits on existing payment infrastructure — users install nothing new, and merchants integrate no new protocol.** That is the mirror image of the U.S. path, where ACP and UCP build protocols from zero: China connected payments first and is adding protocols after; the U.S. is standardizing protocols first and wiring payments in. For cross-market comparison, this is the hardest first-party transaction evidence on the China side.

**Both roads hit the same wall.** Checkout.com's survey concludes that consumers are already trying agents in everyday shopping and the industry is collaborating intensively on the protocols and standards the next stage of commerce needs, but adoption has outrun infrastructure; the gap sits in "trust infrastructure" — payment authorization, liability, refunds and dispute handling have no settled practice when an agent buys on someone's behalf<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-69">[11-69]</a></sup>[third-party verified]. **That is the same coin as the two German rulings in Section 10.16: one side patches authorization and liability at the protocol layer, the other asks in court whose statement the AI's output is.** A machine customer requires two tickets, one protocol and one payment; holding either alone is not enough — the missing one decides whether you are still on the shelf next year.

## 11.13 After Platforms Make "Agent-Readable" a Default Switch

**Start with an easily overlooked engineering difference.** On October 22, 2026 the Korean e-commerce SaaS platform Cafe24 announced WebMCP support in its storefront solution: every D2C store built on it can now support AI agents performing shopping actions without additional development, covering 11 functions including product search, add to cart, direct checkout and order lookup. The path matters. Conventional agents locate page buttons and inputs and simulate clicks; WebMCP lets an agent call preset functions directly in a defined format, which is faster and more accurate. Login and final payment still require the user<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-70">[11-70]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **This fills the gap between protocol layer and merchant layer: not every merchant has to write its own agent interface, because platform-level SaaS is turning agent-readability into a default switch and absorbing the integration cost for smaller merchants. Once shelf-side agent compatibility is standard, competition returns entirely to product information and content.**

**But "can converse" is not "can transact," and that distinction deserves to become a test.** After testing six Chinese AI platforms one by one, Newrank concluded that only Qwen (wired directly into Taobao) and Doubao (wired directly into Douyin Mall) complete a purchase inside the conversation: Doubao searches reviews across the web, gives a conclusion, attaches a purchase link that opens a Douyin Mall product page, and after authorization places the order directly. Kimi positions itself as a neutral shopping guide, recommending Taobao and JD.com products but always jumping to external apps — essentially referral traffic. Tencent Yuanbao attempted a connection to WeChat Mini Shops and has stalled. The rest have not integrated. The article reduces "AI commerce" to two testable conditions: does the platform own a shelf, and can the order be completed inside the conversation. The resulting landscape is a duopoly with different temperaments — Qwen is the guide, clarifying needs before filtering; Doubao is the content matchmaker, conclusion first, transparently labeling the keywords and sources it searched, biased toward efficiency<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-71">[11-71]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **For brands, "which pool do I enter" precedes "do I do GEO at all" — platforms without their own shelf can only create awareness and refer traffic, with conversion still landing in the merchant's own store.**

**Once the loop closes, the accounting changes.** Three things happened in the same week in July 2026: Kimi K3 launched and Qwen and DeepSeek were upgraded in succession; Yuanbao and JD.com fully connected, letting users reach JD product pages and order from inside Yuanbao; and the Doudian back office began identifying orders arriving from the Doubao channel. The third matters most — the hardest part of GEO had been the accounting, since traffic arrived without any visible conversion path. Now AI-channel orders can be reported separately, brands can attribute GMV, and GEO moves from a visibility budget to an accountable channel. The same piece flags two risks: as models get better at spotting low-quality content, tactics built on volume will fail faster; and entry points are spreading from apps to the system layer and new hardware (Apple Intelligence has filed, and Huawei, Xiaomi, OPPO and vivo are all shipping native phone AI, with glasses, watches and car systems following), so brand corpus work has to cover those surfaces early. The underlying requirements compress to three: accuracy (parameters, prices and service scope correct), consistency (one set of names and claims across the web, or the model contradicts itself) and authority (central media, trade media and verified official accounts outweigh anonymous forums)<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-72">[11-72]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The rules at the entry point are being rewritten by regulators too.** On September 11, 2026, seven ministries including the Ministry of Commerce jointly issued a notice whose first item is "organizing quality promotion on e-commerce platforms," requiring platforms to publish quality commitments publicly, issue quality governance reports, establish a chief quality officer system, and build a full-chain quality control system verifiable against government data. The article's reading: platforms are no longer merely traffic distributors but must answer for the explainability of their search ranking — why this product first and not another needs a traceable basis. That shifts on-platform ranking weight from price-plus-sales to quality-plus-reputation-plus-credit, and merchants' operational focus from pushing volume to building quality and credit assets the platform can verify; expect a window in which rules are opaque and enforcement lags<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-73">[11-73]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **For Chapter 11 the implication is that machine-customer admission is decided not only by protocols but by verifiability as defined by regulators.**

## 11.14 The Price of Permission: From Agent Stores to Paid Crawling

**China's agent stores are defined by policy first, then opened to scenarios.** In May 2026 the Cyberspace Administration, the National Development and Reform Commission and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology jointly issued the Implementation Opinions on Standardized Application and Innovative Development of Agents, explicitly calling for "establishing agent software stores and industry supply-demand information platforms, guiding agent companies to publish products and creating cluster effects," with matching supply-demand matchmaking and mechanisms such as public tenders and open challenges to attract customized development. The text defines an agent as "an intelligent system with autonomous perception, memory, decision-making, interaction and execution capabilities," sets out four pillars — a solid technical foundation, security bottom lines, application pull and an innovation ecosystem — and lists 19 typical application scenarios<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-74">[11-74]</a></sup> [official]. Four months later, in September 2026, MIIT issued the Action Plan for "AI Plus Software," calling for building agent software stores and skill-package (Skills) repositories, standardizing listing review and operations, and fostering open-source agent zones<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-75">[11-75]</a></sup> [official]. **Read together, the path is clear: policy defines the store, then opens the scenarios, then installs tiered governance. Early entrants get shelf position and a say in standards rather than mere exposure — a completely different game from long-tail distribution in consumer app stores.**

**The layer above is starting to price access.** Cloudflare turned the choice sites make about AI crawlers into three settings: Allow, Charge, Block. Under Charge, crawler requests that have not expressed willingness to pay receive HTTP 402 with a crawler-price header (for example USD 0.01 per request); crawlers may retry with an exact or maximum price, and Cloudflare settles as Merchant of Record. Crawler identity is verified through Web Bot Auth signatures, closing the hole that let spoofed user agents take content for free. The capability has been in private beta since its July 1, 2025 launch, and site-side configuration currently supports only a single site-wide price per request rather than pricing by content tier; on the ecosystem side, Stack Overflow is an early paying participant, and Sky News, Quora, Raptive and Webflow have publicly endorsed a licensed-crawling framework<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-76">[11-76]</a></sup> [official]. **The practical effect: being citable moves from free by default to potentially gated by price and identity. Once upstream sources are blocked, visibility strategies that depend on the open web stop working, so companies need to separate open citable assets from content reserved for their own channels.**

**The last layer is form factor, which decides where an agent starts the action.** In March 2026 Perplexity's AI browser Comet arrived on the App Store, offering blended search results (web retrieval plus AI summary plus follow-up), voice queries against open tabs, and direct comparison across multiple pages. What matters is how the agent capability is implemented: constrained by iOS, Comet cannot click locally the way the desktop version does and instead drives cloud virtual browser instances — according to in-app notices, asking it to find a product on Amazon and add it to the cart sends the relevant cookies to Perplexity's cloud. The browser is free for users 18 and over, with advanced AI features requiring a subscription (from EUR 22 a month), and it still runs on Apple's WebKit<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-77">[11-77]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Once "ordering on the user's behalf" moves from the local device to a cloud session, merchant device fingerprints, login state and anti-bot rules either fail or misfire. The cost of this direction has a ready comparison: OpenAI's ChatGPT Atlas launched on October 21, 2025 and stopped working on August 9, 2026 — 292 days. Maintaining a browser means filling in extensions, passwords, cookies, input methods, developer tools and security updates; the agent experience was unstable (an early test took about ten minutes to add an item on Amazon, against roughly two for Comet); prompt injection is more direct when an agent can read pages while logged in; and platform coverage never went beyond Apple Silicon Macs. Meanwhile Chrome held 68.22% global share, Safari 16.47% and Edge 5.37%, and none of the new forms appeared in mainstream browser statistics<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-78">[11-78]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **The question this comparison raises is more useful than an answer: does the user want an AI browser, or AI in the browser? Retrofitting existing browsers costs almost nothing in migration, which means a standalone form must deliver an experience leap large enough to justify the switch — and for machine customers the same holds: the entry point can change, but migration cost does not disappear.**

## 11.15 Checkout Returns to the Merchant, Discovery Stays with the Platform

Section 11.10 inventoried the protocol stack and 11.12 covered the merging of carts. This section fills in the full arc of the spring 2026 retreat: three platforms each re-drew the line between "checkout" and "discovery," and once the line was drawn, the merchant's work surface actually became clearer.

**OpenAI shifted from "owning checkout" to "owning the selection."** On March 24, 2026, OpenAI published "Exploring products directly in ChatGPT," turning the shopping experience into visual browsing, side-by-side comparison and live-updating detail pages, rolled out to all Free, Go, Plus and Pro users that week. The technical move was extending the agentic commerce protocol from transactions to product discovery: fuller, more relevant and fresher product information pulled directly into the conversation, with users able to upload an image to find similar items and narrow conditions step by step in dialogue. The official message to merchants is that this brings "higher-intent consumers who are closer to a final purchase decision," with the merchant entry point at chatgpt.com/merchants<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-79">[11-79]</a></sup> [official]. **Translated into action: the entry point moved from "capable of taking payment" to "capable of carrying product data," with payment left to the merchant.**

**Shopify's approach gives that division a concrete shape.** In March 2026, Shopify emailed merchants to announce that "ChatGPT in Agentic Storefronts" would launch later that month and be on by default for all stores, with a switch in the admin. The key change is where checkout happens: in the early pilot buyers could complete Instant Checkout inside ChatGPT with Shopify Payments; under the new arrangement products still appear in the conversation, but purchases usually complete on the merchant's own store — an embedded browser in the ChatGPT mobile app or a new tab on desktop, with the transaction still running through Shopify's checkout and payments infrastructure and orders landing in the merchant's back office as usual. An OpenAI spokesperson said the priority is "doing search and product discovery well and letting ACP serve as the infrastructure connecting users and merchants; Instant Checkout moves into Apps," and clarified that Shopify's email phrase "complete the purchase inside ChatGPT" means "discoverable and purchasable," not OpenAI's instant checkout. Shopify confirmed merchants need not build a ChatGPT app, since eligible products are supplied automatically to AI platforms through Shopify Catalog<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-80">[11-80]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **The timeline matters for understanding who holds the customer relationship: the platform gave up checkout to take back the discovery entry point.**

**Google took the other road, keeping checkout on its own surface.** In January 2026 Google announced checkout inside AI Mode, built on the new Universal Commerce Protocol developed with Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair, Target and Walmart, with more than 20 backers including Adyen, American Express, Best Buy, Mastercard, Stripe, Home Depot and Visa; payment runs through Google Pay and Google Wallet, with PayPal joining later, starting with eligible US retailers. The point is that "the retailer remains the merchant of record, but checkout happens on Google's screen" — the transaction survives, the site visit may not. The simultaneously launched Business Agent puts a brand's own AI support into search results: live from January 12, 2026 with Lowe's, Michael's, Poshmark, Reebok and others, configured by merchants in Merchant Center, officially described as a "virtual salesperson" that answers product questions in the brand's voice, with later support for training on merchant data, promoting offers and closing sales inside the conversation. The third piece, Direct Offers, is an ad pilot in AI Mode where merchants set a dedicated discount in campaign settings and Google decides when to show it, with early partners including Petco, e.l.f., Samsonite, Rugs USA and Shopify merchants<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-81">[11-81]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **Together the three say one thing: the merchant entry point in AI search is moving from "being cited" to "being configured" — catalog attributes, support scripts and promotion rules all have to be pre-set in Merchant Center.**

**And most retailers are not yet in a position to sit at that table.** An audit of top retailers' product pages concludes that 70% currently fail the requirements the commerce protocols set for "AI buying," and are therefore invisible in agentic commerce. The cause is specific: agents evaluate more than basic transaction metadata, looking at fields such as priceValidUntil (whether the price is still valid) and shippingDetails.deliveryTime (delivery time); a missing field means the agent skips the product rather than guessing. The author's judgment is that this is no longer something an SEO team can solve alone — it spans product data, the commerce platform and a board-level case to the CMO, and "the winner is not the loudest brand, nor the best-ranking page, but the retailer that makes it easiest for an agent to place an order"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-82">[11-82]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

## 11.16 The Access Layer: Whoever Plugs the Catalog In Gets Distribution First

**Payments companies worked out the connection before merchants did.** On October 28, 2025, PayPal announced adoption of OpenAI's Agentic Commerce Protocol: ChatGPT users can check out in Instant Checkout with their PayPal balance, card or bank account, and PayPal handles merchant-side processing, buyer and seller protection, tracking and disputes. The key is that PayPal made itself an access layer — feeding merchant catalogs into ChatGPT through its ACP server, handling merchant routing, payment validation and orchestration, so merchants do not each need their own technical integration. That path explains the sequence in which agentic commerce lands: payments players connect their existing merchant networks first, and merchants then do the work of structuring product data. PayPal's stated scale is hundreds of millions of people using ChatGPT weekly and more than 400 million shopping with PayPal, while internally it has rolled ChatGPT Enterprise out to more than 24,000 employees<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-83">[11-83]</a></sup> [official claims].

**The merchant-side technical due-diligence checklist was written by a payments company.** Stripe's technical field guide, published in March 2026, lays out its own infrastructure progress: the open-source ACP specification released over the previous six months, the first complete agentic solution delivered through the Agentic Commerce Suite — live with URBN's Anthropologie, Free People and Urban Outfitters as well as Etsy — and support for machine payments that let agents pay directly for content and data. The most valuable part puts the three questions merchants kept asking across dozens of conversations on the page: which channel to start with, how to be found by agents, and whom to build with. Those three define the entry sequence for agentic commerce — channel choice sets exposure, discoverability depends on product data structure, and partners determine engineering cost<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-84">[11-84]</a></sup> [official]. **Read it as a technical due-diligence checklist, not as marketing material.**

**The price of an answer slot is being pushed up by the platforms' own earnings.** Forrester used the October 2026 earnings round to point at a structural contradiction: Google's AI Mode delivered the fastest year-over-year query growth in five years, yet ad revenue growth slowed to 12%, the lowest in five quarters — AI is decoupling query growth from monetization growth while depressing the referral traffic that funds the open web. The hardest evidence comes from Reddit: its CEO said on the earnings call that search referrals fell "significantly" in the third quarter, attributing it to AI Overviews and AI Mode keeping users inside the answer, with the call disclosing a decline of more than one million referrals a week on average in October. Forrester's next risk is advertising: Google plans to expand ads in AI Mode and AI Overviews, enter international markets for the first time and test multi-turn conversational ads in the US; its own data shows 70% of frequent answer-engine users in the UK and US "trust the answers answer engines give," and once users suspect ads influence answers, that trust feeds back into query growth itself<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-85">[11-85]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. A reading from the same week confirms that expansion is outrunning trust-building: ChatGPT ads expanded to 26 more countries for a total of 89, with more than 100,000 advertisers in the eight months since launch; third-party measurement shows weekly click-through on ChatGPT ads up 112% from late March, with Brazil's historical peak at 1.91%, the US at 0.73%, and Mexico lowest in reachable audience at 27.8%<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-86">[11-86]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **Two implications for brands: answer slots will get more expensive and more ad-like; and being trusted becomes a scarce resource shared by platforms and brands alike.**

## 11.17 The Delegation Ladder: Expectations Rising, Authorization Tightening

**AI taught consumers to start shopping elsewhere; it did not teach them to stop buying from Amazon.** PYMNTS data shows Amazon accounts for 29% of where consumers begin online retail research but 49% of completed purchases, rising to 59% of the most recent AI-assisted purchase. On scale, about 132 million US adults have made an AI-assisted purchase and 58.4 million have started research with an AI tool, but only 62.9 million are willing to let an agent both choose and buy, and 36.8 million are willing to let an AI app hold their card. The most telling comparison: of 57.5 million AI-assisted shoppers who say they use AI to research things they used to search on Amazon, 54.5 million end up ordering on Amazon anyway. The authors call it a delegation ladder rather than a switch — the share willing to let an agent complete a purchase fell from 32% in September 2025 to 24% in August 2026, meaning consumers are grading tasks by risk. On the merchant side: 87% see agentic commerce as an opportunity, 90% have started product and catalog work, and they spend an average of 2% of annual revenue preparing, but only 23% consider themselves "very ready" to connect to AI platforms<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-87">[11-87]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**Put several surveys from the same period side by side and two curves run in opposite directions.** Expectations are rising: Global Payments' annual report says consumers now expect AI agents to complete 15% of purchases within five years, up from 9% on the same question a year earlier; nearly half of Americans (45%) have used or would consider an AI shopping assistant, and comfort with letting AI buy in everyday categories such as movie tickets and food delivery more than doubled in a year. Authorization is tightening: Riskified's survey of 2,000 consumers in the UK and US finds 61.5% already use AI to find products, but 55.0% explicitly refuse to let an agent complete the transaction; Horizon Media's survey of 1,000 US consumers finds 70% willing to use AI to find deals and only 33% accepting AI placing orders directly. Market-size forecasts diverge just as widely: McKinsey puts 2030 at $3–5 trillion globally, Bain at $300–500 billion (15%–25% of US e-commerce), Morgan Stanley at $190–385 billion and Juniper at $1.5 trillion<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-88">[11-88]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. **The gap between expectation and authorization is exactly the hole trust infrastructure has to fill — which is why "who absorbs the loss when a transaction fails" will decide this round more than "who has connected more channels."**

**The last piece of the behavioral picture comes from a retail measurement firm.** NielsenIQ data from May 2026 shows 42% of consumers already use AI tools in shopping, concentrated in product discovery, comparison and selection. Its positioning matters: a company that measures retail and runs consumer panels putting AI usage into routine tracking means "does AI affect purchasing" is moving from a marketing topic to a retail infrastructure metric. For brands, the use of this number is not "AI matters" but judging whether the share of AI-assisted decisions in their own category has crossed the panel's noise floor — above four in ten means it can no longer be dismissed as early-adopter bias<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-89">[11-89]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

## 11.18 A2A Takes Shape in B2B First: One-Fifth of Purchases on 1688 Start with an Agent

**Machine customers gained traction first not in consumer markets but in industrial belts.** Field research across the Yiwu, Yongkang and Keqiao industrial belts yields a demand-side number: more than 20% of purchasing requests on Alibaba's 1688 are now initiated by AI agents, projected to exceed 40% this year and possibly 80% within two years — the machine customer has taken shape on China's largest B2B platform on the buyer side, while sellers use AI in parallel to take orders, test designs and schedule production; the author calls it "B2B becoming A2A." The factory-level math: a Zhejiang household-goods maker now turns out with 200 people the output that used to need 1,000, keeping headcount flat while doubling revenue; one-click image generation produces localized product images for overseas markets in 30 seconds, replacing what used to take "three designers plus two translators" at several hundred thousand yuan a year in fixed labor; a Shaoxing home-textile mill dropped third-party design (saving RMB 50,000–60,000 a year) for AI image testing, reading clicks and inquiries before scheduling production, minimizing the inventory risk of producing before selling; two other factories respectively compressed weeks of process discussion into days with AI modeling and flexible sampling, and moved to an "AI replacing machines" stage after a RMB 5 million integrated robotic cell automated 90% of polishing. The industry baseline has shifted with it: net margins in most categories run only about 5%, 1688 is described by the factory owners interviewed as an "AI-driven capacity dispatch center," and supply-chain response is compressed to within 48 or even 24 hours; meanwhile traditional sampling shops that still rely on old-customer referrals and refuse AI design testing saw order volume nearly halve over the past year<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-90">[11-90]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The downside appears alongside it, and concretely.** One-click image generation instantly flooded the platform with homogeneous AI images and imaginary products, worsening mismatches between listing and goods and blurring the line on design plagiarism. That is exactly the filtering logic of the agent-buyer era: **the qualification to be "chosen by AI" comes from verifiable capacity and lead-time data, not from how beautiful the image is** — the buyer-side agent does not appreciate aesthetics; it reads fields, fulfillment history and reorder rates<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-90">[11-90]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

## 11.19 Readiness, Search and Orders: Three Reconciliations

This chapter has covered protocols, payment rails and distribution rights. This section sets three materials from the second half of 2026 side by side to see how far agentic commerce has actually come — its progress is uneven: companies closer to the transaction move faster, companies that depend on being found move slower.

**The first is the readiness gradient.** Kearney reviewed the public communications of 106 companies across the consumer value chain from September 2025 to August 2026, scoring them on a five-level scale (from making no mention of AI to having agentic commerce live in market) and separating evidence "said to investors" from evidence "said in the company's own voice." The result is a steep curve: all six digital retailers reached at least an explicit agent strategy, and five have live products; of sixteen physical and specialty retailers, eleven reached strategy level and eight are live; of 53 consumer-goods companies only twenty reached strategy level and just two are live; five of 21 luxury players; two of eleven travel retailers. The reason is not hard to see: retailers hold the transaction and the customer interface, can build their own assistants and plug directly into AI channels, while manufacturers depend on being found and sit in a passive position. Supporting market data sharpens the urgency: in Q1 2026 AI-referred traffic to US retail sites grew 393% year on year and converted 42% better than other channels (a year earlier it converted 38% worse); two in five US consumers have shopped with AI, and 85% of them say the experience is better, though two-thirds still want to make the final call themselves; the share of sessions in ChatGPT with commercial intent rose from 13.9% to 19.2% in a year (roughly 28 billion shopping-related conversations annually); Amazon says Rufus drives about $12 billion in annualized incremental sales, and Walmart says Sparky users spend 35% more per order<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-91">[11-91]</a></sup> [third-party verified]. Kearney's advice to manufacturers is to activate assets they already own — recipe libraries, replenishment hardware, diagnostics and health data, age-verification ecosystems — and connect them to where consumers actually decide, rather than starting a new program.

**The second is the shift in the search entry point.** Rufus now answers about 20% of searches on Amazon, and more than 250 million shoppers have interacted with it since rollout. The mechanical difference is the key: traditional search matches keywords against titles, five bullet points, back-end terms and descriptions, then layers on sales and conversion ranking; Rufus reads the full listing, A+ content, individual reviews and Q&A together and judges whether this product can answer this person's question. The object of optimization therefore shifts from keyword density to extractable facts: specific parameters, who it suits, usage scenarios, real experiences that reviews can verify. Reviews and Q&A directly shape what the answer draws on, so post-purchase follow-up and encouraging reviews that describe specific usage details feed back into visibility<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-92">[11-92]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**The third is the order reconciliation.** Shopify's Q3 2026 revenue rose 32% year on year to $3.7 billion and GMV 29.5% to $119 billion; management disclosed that AI-driven traffic grew 2.6x year on year and orders from AI grew 3.3x (faster than traffic), that AI search with a product catalog converts at twice the rate of ordinary AI questions, and that AI channels send shoppers to product pages three times as often as organic search with 64% higher conversion; Shop Pay has been connected to third-party AI interfaces so algorithms can complete payment without touching raw card numbers. But PYMNTS's supplementary data pulls the optimism back: among US consumers using autonomous AI assistants, 40% would rather complete the purchase at a large retailer; nearly 60% of recent AI-assisted purchases happened on Amazon; and 57.9% of people who used AI to find products still placed the order on Amazon. PYMNTS CEO Karen Webster's judgment is quoted as the conclusion — "recommendation is the easy part; certainty and trust are the product"<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-93">[11-93]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

Put the three tables together and this chapter's conclusion gets more specific: **agent channels are now a measured source of increment, but they split being discovered from being transacted.** The first can be optimized (readable facts, citable structure, stable inventory and address data); the second is still decided by payments, fulfillment and trust assets. The slowest end of the readiness curve — consumer-goods manufacturers — happens to hold neither: no transaction interface and no platform-scale trust asset, leaving "being readable" as the only position to compete for.

## 11.20 Entry Points Consolidate, Boundaries Tighten

Sections 11.11 and 11.15 discussed the convergence of distribution entry points and the platform's claim on discovery. In the second half of 2026 both surfaced at once: platforms made entry points fewer and more concentrated, while the party holding fulfillment and safety responsibility began redrawing boundaries through rules.

Start with the entry point. In May 2026 Amazon announced it was retiring its shopping assistant Rufus and folding it into Alexa for Shopping, embedded directly in the main search bar and product detail pages — not a rename but a merger of Rufus's product understanding with Alexa+'s user understanding into one entry point, one dataset and one identity. Six rules have direct consequences for sellers: the search bar moves from keyword search to direct conversation, with buyers asking for "a coffee machine for a small kitchen under $50" and the AI matching answers from across the catalog; AI-generated product overviews appear at the top of results and on detail pages; scheduled actions ("add to cart if it drops to $10," "restock monthly") and direct ordering arrive, with a year of price history viewable, turning price from a static tag into a dynamic variable in AI decisions; conversations and preferences carry across Echo, app and website into a long-term cross-device profile; Shop Direct supports cross-store and sitewide comparison, and Buy for Me can complete purchases at other online stores on the buyer's behalf; and the entry point is free, needing neither Prime nor an Echo device. The actions for sellers follow: write titles and bullets in scenes (audience, space, budget range, usage threshold); replace unverifiable words like "high quality" and "durable" with parameters and facts; keep every section mutually consistent (an outdoor title with indoor-only images will be summarized wrong); avoid irregular price changes that get flagged as unstable; and pre-answer frequent questions in the Q&A section with structured answers<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-94">[11-94]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

The "Rufus answers about 20% of Amazon searches" cited in 11.19 measures how much the capability is used; a product name leaving the stage is not the entry point leaving — it moved up a layer. **Entry points consolidating means the probability of being recommended concentrates further into a handful of systems, and being readable, summarizable and orderable becomes one and the same requirement.**

Now the boundaries. United Airlines announced it is banning third-party personal AI agents from booking flights on customers' behalf, citing security and compliance: sharing account credentials with third-party assistants can expose sensitive information, and actions taken by those tools may cause unintended purchases, account changes or use of miles; checking in via an agent can also conflict with federal rules requiring travelers to verify in person that their baggage contains no prohibited items. Its restriction runs both ways — blocking within its own digital platforms and indirectly through travel agencies and aggregators. Southwest went the other way in the same period, making its flight inventory viewable to ChatGPT users so they can reach the airline's site through the AI app. The analyst reading is blunt: United does not want yet another intermediary, potentially more powerful than search engines, metasearch and OTAs, and flight searches cost airlines money, so AI bot query volume is a cost. Fares remain bookable through aggregators such as Kayak, Hopper and Google Flights, so finding fares will not get harder for consumers — but booking directly through an AI assistant is now closed<sup><a href="12-appendix-a-sources.md#11-95">[11-95]</a></sup> [third-party verified].

**In the same stretch, platforms pulled entry points tighter and the businesses being distributed started drawing harder boundaries.** This is the other face of the judgment in 11.15: transactions can retreat to the merchant's side to complete, but "who has the right to initiate a transaction on the customer's behalf" is being redefined as a permission that must be granted, not a capability that is available by default.

## 11.21 Closing: The Next Decade of Customer Operations

Threading the whole book together, the evolution of AI-powered customer lifecycle management can be summarized in three phases:

**Phase 1 (2023-2025): Tool substitution.** Companies treat AI as a tool that replaces repetitive work — AI support, AI outbound, AI analytics. The winners in this phase are the most efficient companies; the losers are those that optimized customer service as a pure cost center (the Klarna lesson).

**Phase 2 (2025-2027): Process redesign.** Companies rebuild their customer-operations processes around AI — AI-native CRM, CSQL, outcome-based pricing. The winners in this phase are the companies with the cleanest data, because AI's ceiling is set by data quality.

**Phase 3 (2027-): Ecosystem change.** Customers themselves begin to turn into AI — machine customers, agent-to-agent transactions. The winners in this phase are the companies that make their businesses something AI agents can "read."

The three phases are not replacements for one another; they stack. The reality of 2026: most companies are still struggling in Phase 1 (74% stuck in pilots — see Chapter 8), a few leaders have already run into Phase 2, and the wave of Phase 3 has already reached the shore.

What this book can do is lay out the map across the three phases: the engine (Chapters 3-6), the foundation (Chapter 7), the organization (Chapter 8), the industry (Chapter 9), and the boundaries (Chapter 10). The rest of the road, operators must walk themselves.

Finally, back to Klarna from the preface. It went from "AI does the work of 700 support agents" to "we went too far" and then back to hiring people again — a complete cycle in two years. The value of this cycle lies not in its conclusion but in its process: it proved the enormous capacity of AI-powered customer lifecycle management, and it proved that customers are people, not tickets. **The entire art of AI-powered customer lifecycle management is to use the scale of AI to serve every individual person well.**
